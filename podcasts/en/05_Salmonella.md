### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** Some history first. The antigenic structure was originally, and **wrongly**, taken to define the species. That produced a genus with **over 2,000 "species."**

**MAYA:** Then Ewing.

**ALEX:** **Ewing's classification had 3 species**:
- *S. typhi*, pathogenic only for humans;
- *S. choleraesuis* (later *S. enterica*), pathogenic in pigs and occasionally humans, with 1,416 serotypes;
- *S. enteritidis*, causing diarrhea in humans and animals.

**MAYA:** And today?

**ALEX:** **The current classification (Popoff and Le Minor) has 2 species:**
- ***S. enterica***, with **6 subspecies**: *enterica, salamae, arizonae, diarizonae, houtenae, indica*;
- ***S. bongori***.

Serovars keep the names given by the Kauffmann-White scheme, **written in roman type, not italics**: *Salmonella* serovar Typhimurium, *Salmonella* ser. Enteritidis, or simply Typhimurium.

**MAYA:** If you counted serotypes as species?

**ALEX:** You'd have **over 2,390 "species."** *S. enterica* subsp. *enterica* contains the serovars found mainly in **humans and mammals**. The other subspecies and *S. bongori* are found in **cold-blooded animals**.

**MAYA:** And the DNA homology story.

**ALEX:** *Salmonella* and *E. coli* share about **90% DNA–DNA homology**, so they ought to be in the same genus. **Protests from microbiologists and clinicians used to the old names** prevented that.

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- Very widespread in nature, present in **all animal species**: birds, fish, snakes, insects.
- **For *S.* Typhi, humans are the only reservoir**, and the same goes for Paratyphi. With few exceptions (Typhi and Paratyphi in humans, Abortus ovis in sheep, Typhisuis in pigs, Gallinarum/Pullorum in poultry), the other serovars have **no host specificity**.
- Two major sources pollute soil, waste water and surface water: **humans and animals**. They survive in the environment for months or even years. Multiplication in the environment is possible but insignificant.
- **The food link is essential** in human infection: meat, **eggs, especially duck eggs**, milk, and contaminated animal feed meal.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- Gram-negative rods, **2–4 by 0.4–0.6 µm**, motile with **peritrichous flagella**. The exception is serovars **Gallinarum/Pullorum, which are non-motile**. Non-capsulated, non-sporulating.
- They **ferment glucose with gas** (**not *S.* Typhi**), produce acid from **mannitol and sorbitol**, and produce **lysine decarboxylase, arginine hydrolase, ornithine decarboxylase and H₂S** (with some exceptions). They use **citrate as the sole carbon source**.
- **No indole, urease, PAD or acetylmethylcarbinol (VP –)**. They don't degrade adonitol or esculin. **They don't ferment lactose.**

**MAYA:** And survival numbers.

**ALEX:** They multiply between **7 and 48 °C, at pH 4–8**. They're **not very heat-resistant**, killed in **5–7 minutes at 100 °C**. They last about **200 days** on soil or pasture, **10–180 days** in food, and **4 years in egg powder**, which is why egg powder is contraindicated in food. Common disinfectants kill them in **30 minutes to 2 hours**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** The antigenic formula has **three parts: the O antigen, phase 1 and phase 2 of the H antigen**.

- **O antigen**: over **60 different O structures** defining **46 O serogroups**. They're labeled with capital letters (**A…Z**) or only with the factor numbers (**O51…O67**).
- **Vi antigen** (envelope, "virulence"): found in **S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi C**. It's a polysaccharide, a **homopolymer of N-acetylgalactosaminuronic acid**. It **covers and masks the O antigen**, preventing O agglutination. It's **the receptor for the lytic bacteriophages**, which is essential for phage typing of this serovar. It also causes leukopenia, prevents C3b binding, inhibits phagocytosis and allows multiplication inside phagocytes.
- **H antigen**: flagellar, protein, type-determining, with **phase variation**. Most serovars alternately express two types of flagella with different antigenic specificities, phase 1 and phase 2. Since **1986** we've known that some strains can express **three or more**.
- **Antigenic variation by transduction**: loss of H (strains become non-motile), loss of O (colonies go S to R), and partial or total loss of Vi.
- **Fimbrial antigens**: antibodies against **type 1 fimbriae**, which appear in typhoid patients or vaccinees, **can give false positives in quantitative serum analysis**.

**MAYA:** Then the Kauffmann-White scheme, Buiuc 31-14.

**ALEX:** The groups you need:
- **Group A**: Paratyphi A, O 1,2,12; H1 a.
- **Group B**: Paratyphi B and **Typhimurium** (O 1,4,5,12; H1 i; H2 1,2), plus Derby, Agona, Heidelberg.
- **Group C1**: Paratyphi C (6,7,Vi), Choleraesuis, Infantis, Virchow.
- **Group C2**: Newport, Muenchen.
- **Group D1**: **Typhi (O 9,12,Vi; H1 d; no phase 2)**, **Enteritidis (O 1,9,12; H1 g,m)**, Dublin, Gallinarum, Pullorum.
- **Group E1**: Anatum, London.
- **Group F**: Aberdeen.
- Plus groups G, I, L, O, R, S.

**MAYA:** So Typhi: O 9, 12, Vi; H d; monophasic.

**ALEX:** Exactly. Memorize that formula.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- Pathogenic through **multiplication and invasiveness**.
- **Colonization** is a mandatory step, done by adhesins and fimbriae that induce changes in the cell membrane. This was the first proof that a hormone receptor could be affected by bacterial attachment and invasion.
- **Invasion genes**: a set of genes identified in *Salmonella*, some similar to *Yersinia* genes.
- **Intracellular survival**: through surface structures and the synthesis of **over 40 different proteins** that appear in response to phagocytosis. **Vi** helps resist phagocytosis and survive after it (Typhi, Paratyphi C).
- **Endotoxin** (O antigen, LPS): a major role in pathogenicity and in the **general symptoms, fever and shock**.
- **"Enterotoxins"**: in strains that cause food poisoning, with antigenic and biological properties **similar to cholera toxin**.
- *Modern layer*: **pathogenicity islands SPI-1** (enterocyte invasion, type III secretion) and **SPI-2** (survival in the macrophage vacuole).

### Clinical significance: three forms of human salmonellosis

**MAYA:** This is where the pathogenesis comes in.

**ALEX:**

**1. Systemic salmonelloses, the enteric fevers (typhoid and paratyphoid fever).** Well-defined clinical entities with a characteristic course, followed by **lasting immunity**. **Infective dose around 10³.**

The pathogenesis is a chain:
1. Attachment and passage through or between the **ileocecal epithelial cells**.
2. Multiplication in the **submucosa**.
3. Phagocytosis by **macrophages**, where the bacilli **stay alive and multiply** (intestinal lymphoid formations).
4. **Mesenteric lymph nodes**.
5. **Thoracic duct**.
6. **First bacteremia**, which marks **clinical onset**.
7. Invasion of other organs and lymphatic formations.
8. Multiplication in the **reticuloendothelial system** (liver, spleen).
9. **A second, massive bacteremia.**
10. **Elimination through bile and/or urine.**

At the **end of the first week** salmonella appears in feces, and excretion continues into convalescence. **Cholangitis and cholecystitis** can occur, and **the patient can become a carrier after recovery**. The general symptoms (fever, shock) come from **endotoxin**. The digestive ones (anorexia, pain, constipation or diarrhea) come from the intestinal, hepatic and gallbladder injury.

**2. Enteric salmonelloses: enterocolitis, gastroenteritis, infectious-type food poisoning.** The common form, widespread and endemic-epidemic. It's a large part of the bacterial diarrheal syndrome, **mainly in children up to 3 years**. **Infective dose 10⁵–10⁸.**

The mechanism: attachment and adsorption onto epithelial cells of the **terminal small intestine**, penetration into the lamina propria and ileocecal region, multiplication in the **lymphoid follicles** with RES hypertrophy and hyperplasia, PMN accumulation, inflammation and **prostaglandin release**, which stimulates intestinal secretion. The result is **non-bloody diarrhea**, nausea, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain, myalgia and headache. **Contagiousness lasts about 3 months.** Symptoms are more severe in **children under 10** and the immunocompromised (HIV/AIDS). With poor defenses, **secondary localizations** can occur: meningeal, pulmonary, bone.

**3. Salmonella septicemias.** Complications of the enteric forms, generalized after a strong insult in immunodeficient patients: children, and elderly people weakened by chronic or malignant disease. **Recorded mortality 10–20%.**

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

#### 1. Collection and transport

**ALEX:**
- **Digestive infections**: feces (early, before antibiotics), vomit, incriminated food. Cary-Blair.

**MAYA:** And the golden rule for enteric fevers.

**ALEX:** **The golden rule of specimen chronology:**
- **in the first week: blood (blood culture)**;
- **afterwards: feces (stool culture), urine (urine culture), bile, and bone marrow (marrow culture).**

Collect as soon after onset as possible and before antibiotics.

**MAYA:** That follows from the pathogenesis. The first week is bacteremic, and the end of the first week is when it reaches the gut through the bile.

#### 2. Macro and micro

**ALEX:** Macroscopically: **watery, non-bloody** diarrhea. Microscopically: wet mount of feces, where you may see mononuclear granulocytes and inflammatory cells. **No stained smears from stool.**

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:** The **isolation scheme**: inoculate an **enrichment broth** (**sodium acid selenite broth**, or alternatively **Müller-Kauffmann tetrathionate broth**, which grows promptly in 24 h and even faster at 40 °C) **and two selective-differential agars**, **MacConkey and ADCL/XLD**.

**MAYA:** Then?

**ALEX:** After 18–24 h at 35–37 °C, suspect **lactose-negative** colonies appear. To raise the detection rate, the **18–24 h selenite culture is subcultured onto solid media**.

**MAYA:** What does selenite look like?

**ALEX:** The color stays the same while turbidity increases. *Salmonella* can turn selenite **brick-red** at 24–48 h, and it can decolorize Müller-Kauffmann.

**MAYA:** Wilson-Blair.

**ALEX:** **Wilson-Blair** is highly selective, with no lactose in the indicator system. It gives **unmistakable colonies: black, rough, with irregular edges and a metallic halo**. It's hard to prepare, which limits its use, but it's **considered indispensable for investigating *S.* Typhi carriers**.

**ALEX:** Chromogenic media: **Rambach, SM ID**. And a note: some sensitive serovars don't grow or grow poorly on media with a lot of brilliant green.

#### 4. Identification

**4a. Morphology.** Gram-negative rods, 0.6 by 2–4 µm, motile with peritrichous flagella except Gallinarum/Pullorum. Other serovars from clinical isolates can occasionally be non-motile too.

**4b. Culture characters.** **S-type, lactose-negative colonies of 1–3 mm**:
- **colorless and semi-transparent on MacConkey**;
- **colorless with a black center on ADCL**;
- **red, semi-transparent, with a black center on XLD**;
- **black with a metallic halo on Wilson-Blair**.

As cultures "age" they become R colonies. *S.* Paratyphi B can produce M colonies. On routine media and blood agar there's usually no hemolysis. **Careful: *S.* Typhi colonies may lack the black center on ADCL or XLD.**

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + with gas (**no gas in *S.* Typhi**), lactose/sucrose –, **H₂S +**.
- MILF/MIU: motile +, **indole –**, **LDC +**, PAD –, **urease –**.
- **Citrate +**, **VP –**, **ODC +**.

**MAYA:** The Typhi peculiarities.

**ALEX:** These are a must:
- **no gas from glucose**;
- **H₂S in small amounts and late** (± at 48 h);
- **Simmons citrate negative**;
- **ornithine decarboxylase negative**;
- **lysine decarboxylase positive**;
- **motile**.

**MAYA:** And the other exceptions, Table 31-16.

**ALEX:**
- ***S.* Paratyphi A**: **LDC-negative**, and only **10% of strains produce H₂S** in TSI.
- **Typhi and Paratyphi A**: ornithine-negative.
- **Gallinarum and Pullorum**: non-motile.
- **Gallinarum**: no gas from glucose.

**MAYA:** Subspecies differentiation?

**ALEX:** Table 31-15: β-galactosidase, gelatinase, galacturonate, growth in KCN, malonate, mucate, d-tartrate, γ-glutamyl transferase, β-glucuronidase, dulcitol, salicin, sorbitol, lactose, and **lysis by phage O₁**.

**And the rule: if the strain is biochemically *Salmonella*, it goes to the CNRS (Cantacuzino Institute) to determine the serovar.**

**4d. Antigenic characters. This is the defining step.**

**ALEX:** **Serotyping by the Kauffmann-White scheme.**

**O antigens:**
- Bacterial suspensions inactivated by heat (**1 hour at 100 °C**) or by **90° ethanol** (**2 hours at room temperature**).
- Start with **polyvalent "O" mixtures** (polyvalent O I, polyvalent O II). Then identify the group with **monovalent "O" sera**: **AO, BO, DO, EO, LO / CO, FO, GO, HO**.

**H antigens:**
- Suspensions inactivated with **5‰ formalin**, kept at room temperature **at least 6 hours**.
- Use polyvalent "H" mixtures I and II (each with 5–7 monovalent sera). Identify **phase H1 first**, with type-specific sera (**a, b, d, e, i, Z₁₀, Z₂₉; k, r, y, z**), then **phase H2** (complexes **g; e,n; l; Z₄; Z₆**).
- **H agglutinations are done in tubes.**

**Anti-Vi serum:**
- If the strain **agglutinates only with Vi serum**, test it additionally with **Hd serum**. If it agglutinates with both, that's a **presumptive result: "*S. enterica* ser. Typhi"**, and the strain goes to the CNRS.

**MAYA:** The three interpretations.

**ALEX:**
- (a) **agglutinates with polyvalent O**: assign group and serovar;
- (b) **biochemically *Salmonella* but doesn't agglutinate with polyvalent O sera**: complete the tests and send to the CNRS;
- (c) **agglutinates with isotonic saline and with all sera**: that's "***Salmonella enterica* subsp. *enterica*, R form**."

**MAYA:** Practical tips.

**ALEX:** O identification may require prior inactivation (heat, acid or acetone). H identification uses formaldehyde inactivation. **The Vi antigen can interfere with O identification.** For speed, live cultures can be used on the slide, but there's a risk of wrong assignment from cross-agglutination.

**4e. Pathogenicity.** Not routine. Experimental pathogenicity in the mouse is a research tool.

**4f. Virulence.** *Modern layer*: PCR for SPI-1/SPI-2 genes, ***invA*** (the classic target for genus detection), ***viaB*** (Vi) and ***spvC*** (virulence plasmid).

**4g. Phage typing.**

**ALEX:**
- Phage typing has been extended to **over 30 serovars**.
- **In Romania, phage typing is routinely done for Typhi, Paratyphi B, Typhimurium and Enteritidis**, using international phage sets from the **International Centre for Enteric Phage Typing, London**, and for **Gallinarum** with a domestic phage set. Serovars **Anatum and Panama** had national schemes.
- **The Vi antigen is the receptor for the lytic phages** used to type serovar Typhi. There are four categories:
  - **Vi-typeable**;
  - **Vi-degraded**: lysis-sensitive but not assignable to a precise lysovar;
  - **Vi-untypeable**: they have Vi but can't be typed with the international set;
  - **Vi-negative**: untypeable.
- **Over 100 lysovars** are defined for ser. Typhi.
- Cumbersome as it is, phage typing is widely used for its discriminatory power and the stability of the spectra and phage preparations. **In many cases it has proved superior to molecular typing methods.**

**4h. Other typing methods.**

- **Biotyping**: the joint **Kauffmann and Petersen** scheme, with **14 tests**: arabinose, dulcitol, inositol, rhamnose, trehalose, xylose, glycerol cleavage (Stern method), H₂S and gelatinase production, and metabolism of d-, l- and i-tartrate, citrate and sodium mucate. It's useful for separating serovars with identical or very similar antigenic structure:
  - **Paratyphi B / Java** (O:1,4,5,12; H:b; 1,2);
  - **Paratyphi C / Choleraesuis / Typhisuis** (O:6,7; H:c; 1,5);
  - **Newport / Kottbus** (O:6,8; H:e,h; 1,2/1,5).
- **Plasmid typing**, 3 techniques: plasmid profile, plasmid fingerprinting (restriction endonucleases), and identification of virulence plasmid genes. Its value has proved fairly limited, but it's useful especially for distinctions within a serovar.

**4i. New-generation methods.**

- **Ribotyping**: applied to intraserovar typing of strains with a single plasmid. Useful for ser. Typhi and ser. Dublin, or for distinctions within a lysovar. **Not discriminatory enough on its own**. It's recommended together with other molecular methods, such as IS200 fingerprinting.
- **IS200 fingerprinting**: studies the distribution of a chromosomal DNA sequence thought to be specific for *Salmonella*. It discriminates well within serovars that have **no phage typing scheme and no plasmids**, such as Paratyphi B biovar Java and Typhimurium. **For ser. Enteritidis the results were unsatisfactory: only 3 patterns across 27 distinct lysovars.**
- **PFGE**: separates fragments from 10 kb to 6,000 kb, giving the "fingerprint" of the whole genome. Its use is expanding, in combination with ribotyping and IS200 fingerprinting.

**MAYA:** And the unconventional direct detection methods, Table 31-18.

**ALEX:**
- **Metabolic methods**: conductance measurement (**BACTOMETER, MALTHUS, RABIT, BACTRACE**), and metabolism of radiolabeled substrates (**BACTEC**).
- **Immunological methods**: direct and indirect immunofluorescence, latex agglutination (**WELCOLEX SALM**), coagglutination, **ELISA** (QTROL SALM, BIO ENZABED, SALMONELLA TEK, TECTRA SALM VIZUAL, VIDAS ELISA AUTOMATIC), and **piezoelectric immunosensors** (sensitivity **1–5 CFU/g**).
- Advantages: rapid, automated screening. Disadvantages: special media and equipment, cost, and sometimes mediocre specificity.

**MAYA:** And why look for alternatives at all? Table 31-17.

**ALEX:** The drawbacks of conventional methods:
- **long duration, 3–5 days**, which makes the diagnosis late and of no practical use, and keeps food in storage for a long time;
- **low sensitivity, 10²–10³ CFU/g**, which gives false negatives;
- high material consumption;
- the need for skilled labor.

**ALEX:** *Modern layer*:
- **MALDI-TOF identifies the genus, not the serovar.**
- **WGS with *in silico* serotyping** (SeqSero2, SISTR) and cgMLST has progressively replaced PFGE in surveillance networks (PulseNet, ECDC), with simultaneous detection of resistance genes.
- Multiplex PCR from stool (syndromic GI panels). Latex agglutination and PCR directly from the specimen for *S.* Typhi are **methods under study, mentioned in the course**.

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- **In enterocolitis**, an antibiogram is generally recommended only for **patients at extremes of age** or for **resistance surveillance**. Disk diffusion.
- **For every *S.* Typhi isolate, susceptibility testing is recommended.**
- **A key EUCAST rule: *Salmonella* and *Shigella* may look "susceptible" *in vitro* to aminoglycosides, first- and second-generation cephalosporins and cephamycins, but these are clinically ineffective in systemic infections and are not reported as susceptible.** That's because the bacteria survive intracellularly.
- Watch for **fluoroquinolone resistance**. Screen with **pefloxacin or nalidixic acid** for *gyrA* mutations: "nalidixic-resistant" strains respond poorly to ciprofloxacin. Also watch for ESBLs and azithromycin resistance.
- **The first ciprofloxacin-resistant *S.* Typhi was identified in 2005** (also mentioned in the course). The current problems are the **MDR H58 clone and the XDR strains from Pakistan**.

### Serological (indirect) diagnosis

**MAYA:** Now the reason *Salmonella* is special.

**ALEX:** **Typhoid and paratyphoid fever are the only major serological indication among enterobacteria.**

- Some authors consider **no serological test specific, sensitive and fast enough**. **The definitive diagnosis remains isolation and identification of *S.* Typhi.**
- **The Widal reaction** was devised at the **end of the 19th century** and standardized in the **mid-20th century**. **Widal with live cultures is no longer done today.**
- **The current method is quantitative serum analysis by tube agglutination.** Separate series of tubes are used, **one series per known antigen**, with the serum diluted according to the protocol. For suspected typhoid or paratyphoid, **6 series of tubes with known, inactivated antigens** are used:
  - **anti-O: TO (*S.* Typhi), AO (*S.* Paratyphi A), BO (*S.* Paratyphi B)**;
  - **anti-H: d (*S.* Typhi), a (*S.* Paratyphi A), b (*S.* Paratyphi B)**.

**MAYA:** Incubation conditions?

**ALEX:**
- **Anti-O tubes: 18–20 hours at 52 °C**, then 10 minutes at room temperature.
- **Anti-H tubes: 2 hours at 52 °C**, then 10 minutes at room temperature.
- Then the reaction is read.

**MAYA:** Titers.

**ALEX:** **Suggestive titers: 1/250 for anti-O and 1/2,500 for anti-H.** A **fourfold rise** between two successive determinations, **7–10 days apart**, confirms the suspicion.

**MAYA:** And the vaccinated?

**ALEX:** **In previously vaccinated people serology can NOT be used**, because the results can't be interpreted. **Isolation in culture is strictly necessary.**

**MAYA:** Anti-Vi?

**ALEX:** **Anti-Vi antibodies are recommended both in patients and, above all, in convalescents and carriers**, by tube agglutination. **A titer above 1/40 can be considered suggestive.**

**MAYA:** And the immune response?

**ALEX:** Anti-O antibodies (IgM, which appear first) and anti-Vi antibodies are protective. Secretory IgA can prevent attachment to the intestinal epithelium.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Salmonella enterocolitis is self-limiting and is NOT treated with antibiotics.** Antibiotics **prolong carriage and the excretion period, increase the frequency of carriage, and select resistant strains**. Antibiotics are used only for **complications, chronic disease, immunosuppression or extremes of age** (ampicillin, Biseptol, chloramphenicol). **Fluid and electrolyte rebalancing is essential.**
- **Typhoid fever** is treated pathogenically and with antibiotics: **ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin or azithromycin**, until the antibiogram is available.
- **Prevention**: hygiene (handwashing, proper food preparation) and **periodic stool-culture checks of food-industry staff**.
- **Three types of typhoid vaccine, with 50–80% protection:**
  1. **Vi polysaccharide vaccine (i.m.)**;
  2. **oral live attenuated Ty21a vaccine**;
  3. **inactivated whole-cell vaccine, the most reactogenic**.

### Recap

**MAYA:**
- Two species, *S. enterica* (6 subspecies) and *S. bongori*. Serovars in roman type.
- Lactose –, H₂S +, citrate +, LDC +, ODC +, indole –, urease –, VP –. Typhi has no gas, little late H₂S, citrate –, ODC –. Paratyphi A is LDC – and only 10% H₂S +.
- Kauffmann-White: Typhi = O 9,12,Vi; H d. Vi masks O and is the phage receptor.
- Typhoid: dose 10³, two bacteremias, blood in week 1, then stool, urine, bile, marrow. Enteritis: dose 10⁵–10⁸, no antibiotics.
- Selenite or Müller-Kauffmann, plus MacConkey and ADCL/XLD. Wilson-Blair for carriers.
- Serology: 6 tube series (TO, AO, BO; d, a, b), 52 °C, titers 1/250 O and 1/2,500 H, a fourfold rise, useless after vaccination. Anti-Vi >1/40 in carriers.
- Phage typing: over 100 Typhi lysovars. EUCAST: don't report aminoglycosides or first- and second-generation cephalosporins as active.
