### Part 1 — Taxonomy: from "family" to "order"

**MAYA:** Why does my exam topic say "the order *Enterobacterales*" and not "the family *Enterobacteriaceae*"?

**ALEX:** Because the taxonomy changed. Some history first. The old family *Enterobacteriaceae* grew enormously. In 1974 it had 12 genera and 36 species. By 1994 it had 30 genera and 107 species. Today we count more than 40 genera and over 150 species of medical significance.

**MAYA:** A few historical dates are worth memorizing.

**ALEX:** Yes. The family name was proposed in 1937. *Serratia marcescens* was described in 1823, which makes it one of the oldest. *Salmonella* Typhi was described in 1884, and *Yersinia pestis* in 1894. Bergey's Manual of 2004 listed 44 genera, of which 25 were significantly involved in human disease.

**MAYA:** And then came 2016.

**ALEX:** Then came Adeolu, 2016. He proposed the order *Enterobacterales*, divided into several families. You don't need every detail, but you should know the main split:
- ***Enterobacteriaceae*** in the strict sense: *Escherichia, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Citrobacter, Cronobacter*;
- ***Yersiniaceae***: *Yersinia* and *Serratia*;
- ***Morganellaceae***: *Proteus, Morganella, Providencia*;
- ***Hafniaceae***: *Hafnia* and *Edwardsiella*;
- plus *Erwiniaceae, Pectobacteriaceae* and *Budviciaceae*.

**MAYA:** So *Serratia* now sits with *Yersinia*, and *Proteus* has its own family with *Morganella* and *Providencia*.

**ALEX:** Exactly. That's why the exam subject is called the "order".

### Part 2 — The practical clinical classification

**MAYA:** Buiuc also has a more practical classification, Table 31-3, based on clinical significance.

**ALEX:** It's very useful at the bench. There are three groups.

**Group I, pathogenic.** These are significant whenever they are isolated: *Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia*, and the diarrheagenic and uropathogenic phenotypes of *E. coli*.

**Group II, conditionally pathogenic.** These are significant when found in uncontaminated specimens, such as CSF, blood cultures or closed collections: *Citrobacter, Enterobacter, Escherichia* (at other sites), *Klebsiella, Morganella, Proteus, Providencia, Serratia*.

**Group III, occasionally pathogenic.** *Cedecea, Hafnia, Kluyvera, Leminorella, Pantoea, Tatumella, Xenorhabdus, Yokenella*.

**MAYA:** So an *E. coli* in stool isn't automatically a problem, but the same *E. coli* in CSF absolutely is.

**ALEX:** Precisely. The specimen gives the isolate its meaning.

### Part 3 — Habitat

**MAYA:** Where do they live?

**ALEX:** Everywhere, but above all in the intestinal microbiota of humans and animals. Feces contain more than 10¹⁰ enterobacteria per gram. There's a classic exam trap here, though.

**MAYA:** Go on.

**ALEX:** More than 90% of the normal intestinal flora is made up of anaerobes, not enterobacteria. Enterobacteria are abundant, but they're a minority.

**MAYA:** Noted. And outside the gut?

**ALEX:** They spread widely by the fecal route into soil, water, plants and animals. *Escherichia, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Serratia* and *Proteus* are routinely found in natural environments. They're part of the biological recycling ecosystems.

**MAYA:** But not all of them.

**ALEX:** Right. *Salmonella* serovars Typhi and Paratyphi, and *Shigella*, have a restricted ecology. They live only in humans and, occasionally, in surface water and soil contaminated from that same human source.

**MAYA:** And in urinary tract infections there's a ranking.

**ALEX:** Yes, worth memorizing in order: ***Escherichia* > *Klebsiella* spp. > *Enterobacter* spp. > *Proteus mirabilis* > *Citrobacter* spp. > *Serratia marcescens*.**

### Part 4 — General characters

**MAYA:** Describe an enterobacterium for me.

**ALEX:** A Gram-negative rod, about 2–3 µm by 0.6 µm, with rounded ends. Under the light microscope you cannot tell the species apart. They're non-sporulating. They're either motile with peritrichous flagella, like *Salmonella*, *Proteus* and *E. coli*, or non-motile, like *Shigella*, *Klebsiella* and *Y. pestis*. Some are capsulated, the classic one being *K. pneumoniae*.

**MAYA:** Growth requirements?

**ALEX:** Undemanding. They grow on simple peptone media, even in the presence of bile. They're aerobic and facultatively anaerobic, and you get growth overnight at 35–37 °C.

**MAYA:** And the biochemical definition. This is the core.

**ALEX:** This is the core. Enterobacteria:
1. **ferment glucose**, with or without gas;
2. are **oxidase-negative**;
3. are **catalase-positive**;
4. **reduce nitrates to nitrites**.

**MAYA:** Oxidase-negative is the big one, because it will separate them from *Vibrio*, *Aeromonas* and *Pseudomonas* later on.

**ALEX:** Exactly. And then there's lactose. Lactose fermentation splits the group into two practical halves:
- **lactose-positive**: *E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter*, and *Serratia* late;
- **lactose-negative**: *Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus, Providencia, Morganella, Yersinia*.

**MAYA:** What do the colonies look like?

**ALEX:** You get S, R or M colonies. S colonies are smooth. R colonies are "old," rough. M colonies are mucoid, from capsulated species. On nutrient agar, S colonies are 2–3 mm, round, domed and moist. R colonies are flat, with a rough surface and irregular margins. Mucoid colonies are 4–5 mm and typical of *Klebsiella* and sometimes *Escherichia*. Some colonies are pigmented: red for *Serratia*, yellow for *Enterobacter* and *Pantoea*. And *Proteus* shows invasive growth in concentric waves, called swarming.

**MAYA:** And in broth?

**ALEX:** S forms give uniform turbidity. R forms give turbidity with a ring or a veil at the surface and a deposit at the bottom.

### Part 5 — Antigenic structure

**MAYA:** Now the antigens. This always feels dense to me.

**ALEX:** There are three main antigens used for identification: **O** (somatic), **K** (envelope or capsular) and **H** (flagellar). Let's take them one at a time.

**The O antigen.** This is the polysaccharide portion of the lipopolysaccharide in the outer membrane. LPS has three parts:
1. **Lipid A.** It's similar in all enterobacteria and carries the toxic activity. It's made of phosphorylated glucosamine disaccharide units with long-chain fatty acids. One example is β-hydroxymyristic acid, with 14 carbons, which occurs in nature only here.
2. **The core polysaccharide.** Similar in all Gram-negative bacilli.
3. **The O-specific chains.** Long, branched, made of repeating oligosaccharide units of 4–6 monosaccharides, often ending in dideoxyhexoses: paratose, colitose, tyvelose, abequose. Some of these occur only in enterobacteria.

**MAYA:** And the physical properties of O.

**ALEX:** It's heat-stable: it survives one hour at 100 °C. It's also acid-resistant and alcohol-resistant, but sensitive to formalin. It's a "group" antigen. O agglutination is slow, taking 1–3 minutes on a slide or 20 hours in a tube. It's fine, granular and adherent, and hard to break up.

**MAYA:** And genetics?

**ALEX:** Synthesis is chromosomally coded. Mutations produce degraded "R" antigens. Those strains become O-inagglutinable and autoagglutinate in saline or in 1/500 acriflavine. The O antigen is equivalent to the endotoxin. The immune response to it is mainly IgM.

**MAYA:** Cross-reactions?

**ALEX:** Plenty. *E. coli* and *Shigella* share O antigens. There are cross-reactions among *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Providencia* and *Salmonella*. Some *E. coli* O antigens even react with *Vibrio*, with blood group antigens, or with animal cell surface antigens.

**The K antigen** is the capsular or envelope antigen. It's a linear polysaccharide, part of the glycocalyx, with repeating units. When it forms a continuous envelope it **masks the O antigen** and inhibits O agglutination. Its specificity is less strict than O. The notation varies: "K" in *Klebsiella* and *Escherichia* (with variants L, A, B), "B" in *Shigella*, "Vi" in *Salmonella* Typhi and Paratyphi C.

**MAYA:** They're called "virulence antigens," right?

**ALEX:** Yes, because they cause leukopenia, allow survival inside macrophages, and increase experimental lethality. They're detected by slide or tube agglutination, with live or heat-killed cultures. In *Klebsiella* they're detected by the **capsular swelling reaction**.

**The H antigen** is flagellar. It's made of flagellin, a protein over 40,000 in molecular weight, structurally similar to myosin. It's absent in non-motile species: *Shigella*, *Klebsiella*, and *Salmonella* Gallinarum/Pullorum. It's **heat-labile**, destroyed at 80–100 °C, denatured by 50° alcohol, but **resistant to formalin**. That's why H suspensions are preserved with 5‰ formalin. It's strongly immunogenic, with an IgG response, and strictly type-specific, so it defines serotypes within O groups.

**MAYA:** And H agglutination looks different.

**ALEX:** Completely different. It's fast, 1 minute on a slide or 2 hours in a tube. The clumps are voluminous and fluffy and break apart easily when shaken. Compare that with O: slow, fine, granular and adherent.

**MAYA:** Phase variation?

**ALEX:** That's in *Salmonella*. The bacterium alternately expresses two sets of genes: H phase 1 and H phase 2. A culture can be all phase 1, all phase 2, or, commonly, a mixture. But any single bacterial cell expresses only one phase at a time.

**MAYA:** Fimbrial antigens?

**ALEX:** F antigens. Most enterobacteria have protein fimbriae, of six types. In *E. coli*, F1 to F12 are described. The complete antigenic formula is written like *E. coli* O55:K5:H21, or the famous O157:H7.

### Part 6 — Virulence factors

**MAYA:** What makes them pathogenic?

**ALEX:** Here's the list from the document:

- **Adhesins** (fimbriae or pili). They're essential for attaching to mucosa, which is the first step of colonization.
- **Endotoxin** (the O antigen, LPS). It's released when the cell wall is destroyed. Experimentally it causes fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, DIC, complement activation and the release of pro-inflammatory vasoactive substances. Massive release leads to endotoxic shock.
- **Exotoxins**:
  - Shiga toxin of *S. dysenteriae* type 1;
  - Shiga-like toxins, or verotoxins, of STEC/EHEC;
  - LT (heat-labile, similar to cholera toxin) and ST (heat-stable) of ETEC;
  - α-hemolysins, which are secreted exotoxins, and β-hemolysins, which are cell-bound and inhibit phagocytosis and chemotaxis.
- **Iron acquisition**: siderophores such as aerobactin, enterobactin and yersiniabactin. They chelate iron, and the bacterium takes the complex back up.
- **Capsular structures**:
  - the **K1 antigen of *E. coli***, a polymer of N-acetylneuraminic acid. It resembles host structures, so it's poorly immunogenic and reduces opsonization and phagocytosis;
  - the *Klebsiella* capsule;
  - the Vi antigen of *S.* Typhi, linked to invasiveness.
- **Plasmids**. They transfer both antibiotic resistance (the R factor) and virulence factors.
- **Type III secretion systems**, the "injectisome": SPI-1/SPI-2 in *Salmonella*, Mxi-Spa in *Shigella*, Ysc/Yop in *Yersinia*. Plus **pathogenicity islands (PAIs)**.

### Part 7 — Clinical significance

**MAYA:** Which genus causes what?

**ALEX:** Buiuc Table 31-2 grades frequency of involvement:
- **intestinal infections**: *Escherichia, Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia* (+++);
- **genitourinary**: *Escherichia, Proteus* (+++);
- **respiratory**: *Klebsiella* (+++);
- **systemic**: *Salmonella, Yersinia pestis* (+++).

**MAYA:** And a few one-liners.

**ALEX:** *Shigella* is the intestinal pathogen *par excellence*. *Serratia, Morganella* and *Proteus* are almost exclusively extraintestinal pathogens. In hospital-acquired musculoskeletal and skin infections, *E. coli, Serratia* and *Enterobacter* rank right behind staphylococci and pseudomonads. At least a third of pneumonias that come from infections of the cavities connected to the upper respiratory tract are caused by enterobacteria.

**MAYA:** And bloodstream infections?

**ALEX:** *S.* Typhi, *S.* Paratyphi and *Y. pestis* always cause severe septicemic infections, though they're rare. What has become common is bacteremia and septicemia with *Escherichia, Enterobacter* and *Klebsiella*.

### Part 8 — Direct laboratory diagnosis: the general scheme

**MAYA:** Now the five steps, as they apply to all enterobacteria.

#### Step 1 — Collection and transport

**ALEX:** Specimens include feces, urine for **quantitative** urine culture, blood cultures, bone marrow, aseptic punctures, closed collections, bile, CSF, wound swabs, necropsy samples, suspect food and vomit.

**MAYA:** The general rules?

**ALEX:** Collect as close to onset as possible, **before antibiotics**, with asepsis, and transport quickly. For feces, the transport medium is **Cary-Blair**, which is bacteriostatic. Urine must reach the lab within **30 minutes**, or be stored at +4 °C. Contaminated specimens are pre-processed by homogenization, grinding or centrifugation.

#### Step 2 — Macroscopic and microscopic examination

**ALEX:** Macroscopically, you look at stool appearance: watery or "rice-water," muco-purulent with blood, or melena. You look at urine (cloudy, purulent), at sputum ("redcurrant jelly"), and at pus.

**MAYA:** And here's a rule I always forget.

**ALEX:** **You do not make stained smears from feces.** You make a native wet mount between slide and coverslip, called the coprocytogram, at 40× objective. You look for PMNs, red cells and epithelial cells. For sputum, pus, CSF and urine sediment, you do Gram and methylene blue smears. You'll see Gram-negative rods, and in *Klebsiella* the capsule appears as a halo.

**MAYA:** And microscopy cannot identify genus or species.

**ALEX:** No. It orients the diagnosis and confirms the quality of the specimen.

#### Step 3 — Culture

**ALEX:** The media, in order of selectivity:
- **Weakly selective**: MacConkey, EMB/Levine, Istrati-Meitert (AABTL).
- **Moderately selective**: ADCL (DCA), SS (*Salmonella-Shigella*), XLD, Hektoen.
- **Highly selective**: Wilson-Blair (brilliant green and bismuth sulfite agar), brilliant green broth.
- **Enrichment**: sodium acid selenite broth, Müller-Kauffmann tetrathionate broth (for salmonellae).
- **Chromogenic**: SMID, Rambach, CHROMagar Orientation, UriSelect, chromID ESBL/CARBA.

**MAYA:** One combination is mandatory.

**ALEX:** At isolation, you must pair a moderately selective medium (MacConkey or EMB) with **blood agar**. Incubate 18–24 hours at 35–37 °C.

**MAYA:** And how many colonies do you pick?

**ALEX:** If there are lactose-negative colonies, subculture 3–5 of them. If all colonies are lactose-positive, take at least 10. They go onto the **TSI + MILF/MIU** combination.

#### Step 4 — Identification

**4a. Morphological and staining characters.** Gram-negative rods, 2–3 by 0.6 µm, non-sporulating. Capsulated in *Klebsiella*, pleomorphic in *Proteus*, bipolar staining in *Yersinia*. Motility is checked on a wet mount or on MIU/MILF medium.

**4b. Cultural characters.** Colony type (S/R/M), size, pigment, lactose-positive or negative on selective media, hemolysis on blood agar, swarming (*Proteus*), and characteristic odor.

**4c. Biochemical characters.** These are central.

**ALEX:** The most commonly used conventional multitest systems in Romania are **TSI (Triple Sugar Iron)** plus either **MIU** (Motility-Indole-Urease) or **MILF** (Motility-Indole-Lysine decarboxylase-Phenylalanine deaminase). Together they let you assign the genus in **18–20 hours**.

**MAYA:** What does each one read?

**ALEX:** TSI reads acid in the butt (glucose), gas, H₂S, and lactose or sucrose on the slant. MILF and MIU read motility, indole, lysine decarboxylase, phenylalanine deaminase and urease. Simmons citrate is added as a supplement.

**MAYA:** Then the preliminary scheme, Buiuc Table 31-6.

**ALEX:** In short:
- ***Escherichia***: acid +, gas +, H₂S –, lactose +, motile, indole +.
- ***Shigella***: gas –, lactose –, non-motile, indole variable.
- ***Salmonella***: gas +, H₂S +, lactose –, motile, indole –, citrate +.
- ***Klebsiella***: gas +, lactose +, non-motile, urease +, citrate +.
- ***Proteus***: H₂S +, lactose –, motile, urease +, PAD (phenylalanine deaminase) +.
- ***Yersinia***: gas –, motile only at 22–30 °C.

**MAYA:** Then the microtest systems.

**ALEX:** Ready-made strips with 12, 20 or 100 tests: API 10 S, API 20 E, API Rapid 20 E, ID 32E. And automated systems: VITEK 2, BD Phoenix, MicroScan WalkAway. They give a numerical code and a probabilistic identification in 4–6 hours.

**MAYA:** And the extended reference set?

**ALEX:** 25–26 tests, Buiuc Table 31-5. They are: indole, methyl red, Voges-Proskauer, urease, lysine, arginine and ornithine decarboxylases, phenylalanine deaminase, H₂S, gas from glucose, adonitol, dulcitol, m-inositol, lactose, mannitol, salicin, sucrose, xylose, β-galactosidase (ONPG), malonate, gelatin, growth in KCN, Simmons citrate, DNase, and motility at 36 °C.

**4d. Antigenic characters.**

**ALEX:** Complete antigenic typing is done only in national or international reference centres. Clinical labs do serotyping for *Salmonella, Shigella, Escherichia* and *Yersinia*.

**MAYA:** The order matters.

**ALEX:** It does. First you determine the **O factors** by slide agglutination, using antigens inactivated by heat (1 hour boiling at 100 °C) or by alcohol (85° ethanol, 2 hours). Then you determine the **H factors** by tube agglutination, with young broth cultures treated with 5‰ formalin for at least 6 hours. **K antigens** are done by slide or tube agglutination, with live or heat-killed cultures.

**MAYA:** Which serovars are kept in practice?

**ALEX:** The clinically and epidemiologically important ones: *Salmonella* Typhi, Paratyphi A and B, Typhimurium and Enteritidis; *E. coli* EPEC, ETEC and EHEC; *Shigella*; and *Yersinia*: *Y. enterocolitica* O3 and O9, and *Y. pseudotuberculosis* type I.

**MAYA:** And latex agglutination?

**ALEX:** It's a rapid screening method, used directly on the specimen or on colonies from isolation media. It works remarkably well for *Shigella*, with a sensitivity of 10^4.5 organisms per ml.

**4e. Pathogenicity characters.**

**ALEX:** *In vivo* tests:
- the **Sereny test**, keratoconjunctivitis in the guinea pig, for *Shigella* and EIEC;
- intragastric inoculation of the newborn mouse, for ETEC;
- the ligated rabbit ileal loop, for ETEC;
- lethal hemorrhagic enterocolitis in the piglet, for EHEC;
- inoculation of the white mouse, for *Klebsiella* and *P. aeruginosa*.

*In vitro* tests: cytopathic effect on **Vero** cells (verotoxins); **HeLa** cells (invasion for EIEC, aggregative adherence for EAggEC, diffuse adherence for DAEC); **Hep-2** cells (A/E lesions for EPEC); and **Caco-2** cells.

**4f. Virulence characters.**

**ALEX:** Direct detection of the virulence factors themselves. Toxins are detected by ELISA, by latex agglutination, by **VET-RPLA** (reversed passive latex agglutination), by immunodiffusion (the **Biken test**), and by seroneutralization of the cytotoxic effect on Vero cells.

Genes are detected by PCR: *elt/est* (LT/ST), *stx1/stx2*, *eaeA* (intimin), *espB*, *bfpA*, *ipaH / ipaB-C* (invasiveness), and the *afa* gene cluster (afimbrial adhesins).

You can also determine plasmid profiles and virulence plasmids: pYV in *Yersinia*, the invasion plasmid in *Shigella*, EAF in EPEC.

**4g. Phage typing (lysotyping).**

**ALEX:** This is a fine-resolution epidemiological marker used to pin down outbreaks. International sets exist for *S.* Typhi and Paratyphi B, from the International Centre for Enteric Phage Typing in London, with over 100 lysovars. There are sets for *S.* Typhimurium and *S.* Enteritidis. *S.* Gallinarum uses a domestic set. There are also schemes for *Shigella flexneri* 2a and *S. sonnei*, for *E. coli* O157 and the EPEC serogroups O26, O55 and O111, and for *Klebsiella*. The *Klebsiella* scheme at the Cantacuzino Institute is the combined Slopek-Milch scheme, with the domestic Popovici scheme added. There are also schemes for *Serratia marcescens* and *Yersinia enterocolitica*.

**MAYA:** And the Vi detail.

**ALEX:** In *S.* Typhi, **the Vi antigen is the receptor for the lytic phages**. Based on typeability, strains are classified as Vi-typeable, Vi-degraded, Vi-untypeable and Vi-negative. And although it's laborious, lysotyping keeps its discriminatory power, which is sometimes better than molecular methods.

**4h. Other typing methods.**

- **Biotyping.** Combinations of biochemical tests that define distinct behavioral types. Example: the Kauffmann–Petersen scheme with 14 tests for *Salmonella*. Those tests are arabinose, dulcitol, inositol, rhamnose, trehalose, xylose, glycerol (Stern method), H₂S, gelatinase, d-, l- and i-tartrate, citrate and mucate. Biotyping is of little use in *Shigella* because its metabolic activity is modest, though ONPG and ornithine decarboxylase do separate *S. sonnei*.
- **Bacteriocin typing.** Colicin typing, promoted for *S. sonnei* and *Serratia*. In *Y. pestis*, pesticins I and II.
- **Resistotyping**, typing by antibiotic pattern. It's accessible and widely used in epidemiology, but it depends on many variables. Plasmid profiling has replaced it, to good advantage.
- **Plasmid typing**: plasmid profile, plasmid fingerprinting with restriction endonucleases, and identification of plasmid virulence genes.

**4i. New-generation methods.**

- **Ribotyping**, useful within a serovar, for example *S.* Typhi or *S.* Dublin. On its own it isn't discriminatory enough.
- **IS200 fingerprinting**: the distribution of the insertion element IS200 in the genome. It discriminates well within serovars that have no phage typing scheme and no plasmids.
- **PFGE** (pulsed-field gel electrophoresis). It separates DNA fragments from 10 kb to over 6,000 kb, giving a "fingerprint" of the whole genome. It was the classic gold standard for outbreak investigation.
- **REAP/REAC**: restriction endonuclease analysis of plasmid or chromosomal DNA. **MLEE**: multilocus enzyme electrophoresis. **RFLP**.
- **MLST** (multilocus sequence typing).

**ALEX:** Now the modern layer:
- **MALDI-TOF MS** identifies the species in minutes, straight from a colony. It's become the routine method and has largely replaced biochemical galleries. Its limits: **it can't separate *E. coli* from *Shigella***, and it doesn't separate the species within the *E. cloacae* complex well.
- **Multiplex PCR, or syndromic GI panels** (FilmArray GI, Seegene, xTAG). They detect *Salmonella*, *Shigella*/EIEC, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*, *Vibrio*, STEC (*stx1/stx2*), ETEC, EPEC and EAEC simultaneously, along with viruses and parasites, directly from stool in 1–2 hours.
- **Whole-genome sequencing with cgMLST/wgMLST.** It has progressively replaced PFGE in surveillance (PulseNet, ECDC). It allows *in silico* serotyping (SeqSero, SISTR) and detection of resistance genes (ResFinder, CARD) and virulence genes at the same time.
- **16S rRNA sequencing**, for isolates that are hard to identify phenotypically.

#### Step 5 — Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

**ALEX:** The standardized method is disk diffusion on **Mueller-Hinton**. MICs are determined by broth microdilution or gradient tests, or with automated systems. In Romania and the EU, the **EUCAST** standard applies, with breakpoint tables updated every year. The categories are S, I ("susceptible, increased exposure"), and R.

**MAYA:** The natural resistances. You keep saying this is a quality-control tool.

**ALEX:** It is. If a lab reports a species "susceptible" to a drug it's naturally resistant to, either the identification or the test is wrong. The expected phenotypes:
- ***Klebsiella*** and ***Citrobacter koseri***: aminopenicillins.
- ***Enterobacter*, *Citrobacter freundii*, *Serratia*, *Morganella*, *Providencia*, *Hafnia***: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first-generation cephalosporins and cefoxitin, because of **inducible chromosomal AmpC**.
- ***Proteus*, *Morganella*, *Providencia***: colistin, tetracyclines, tigecycline, nitrofurantoin.
- ***Serratia***: colistin.
- ***P. mirabilis***: nitrofurantoin.

**MAYA:** And the acquired mechanisms to watch for?

**ALEX:**
- **ESBLs** (extended-spectrum β-lactamases: CTX-M, SHV, TEM). They're detected by the **double-disk synergy test**, cefotaxime or ceftazidime with and without clavulanic acid.
- **AmpC hyperproduction**: cefoxitin resistant, synergy with cloxacillin.
- **Carbapenemases**: KPC, OXA-48, NDM, VIM, IMP. They're confirmed by the Carba NP test, mCIM/eCIM, rapid immunochromatographic tests or PCR.

The newer combinations are ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-relebactam, and cefiderocol.

### Part 9 — Serological (indirect) diagnosis

**MAYA:** What about serology in enterobacteria generally?

**ALEX:** The general rule is that **diagnosis is bacteriological, meaning direct**. There's a post-infection humoral response, but in most genera it's neither protective nor diagnostically useful.

**MAYA:** Exceptions?

**ALEX:** Three with practical value:
1. **Typhoid and paratyphoid fever**: quantitative Widal-type serum analysis, and anti-Vi antibodies in carriers;
2. **Yersiniosis**, for extraintestinal manifestations: reactive arthritis, erythema nodosum;
3. **Plague**.

**MAYA:** And the trick with *Proteus*.

**ALEX:** A "positive *Proteus* serology" is the **Weil-Felix reaction**, with strains OX-19, OX-2 and OX-K. It actually diagnoses **rickettsial diseases**, not *Proteus* infection.

### Part 10 — Treatment and prevention: general principles

**ALEX:** Treatment is guided by the antibiogram. In acute diarrheal disease, **fluid and electrolyte rebalancing is essential**.

**MAYA:** And antibiotics in diarrhea?

**ALEX:** In most bacterial acute diarrheal diseases, antibiotics are **not** indicated. The exceptions are bacillary dysentery, cholera, enteric fevers, severe or invasive forms, patients at extremes of age, and the immunocompromised.

**MAYA:** Prevention?

## Episode 2 — *Escherichia coli*: Commensal, Opportunist and Six-Faced Intestinal Pathogen

*Hosts: **ALEX** and **MAYA***

---

**[INTRO MUSIC]**

**MAYA:** Welcome back to *The Gram-Negative Rounds*. Today's episode is about the single most-isolated organism in any clinical lab: *Escherichia coli*.

**ALEX:** The workhorse. It's in your gut right now, it's the number-one cause of urinary tract infections, and it can also cause six different kinds of diarrhea.

**MAYA:** Let's go through it in the document's order.

---

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The type species is *Escherichia coli*. It was first described in 1885 as *Bacterium coli commune* by Theodor Escherich, and the current name dates from 1919. Other species in the genus: *E. blattae*, found only in insects; *E. fergusonii*; *E. hermannii*; *E. vulneris*; and *E. albertii*, described in 2003.

**MAYA:** And the relationship with *Shigella*.

**ALEX:** DNA–DNA hybridization shows a very close relationship with *Shigella*. Some authors, Holt in 1994 for example, put them into a single genus, *Escherichia-Shigella*. Bergey keeps them separate.

**MAYA:** And the "inactive" *E. coli*.

**ALEX:** That's the trap. There's an "inactive" type of *E. coli* that is non-motile, lactose-negative and lysine decarboxylase-negative. In practice it's very hard to tell apart from *Shigella*.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** It's part of the normal intestinal microbiota of humans and warm-blooded animals, and less often of the upper respiratory or genital flora. It helps synthesize some vitamins, so it's a symbiont. It's ubiquitous in water and soil, and it's **the indicator of fecal pollution of water**.

**MAYA:** So when does it become a pathogen?

**ALEX:** In two ways. Either it **changes habitat**, for example it reaches the urinary tract, or it **acquires pathogenicity factors**, which gives us the pathotypes.

### General characters

**ALEX:** It's a straight or slightly curved Gram-negative rod, 2–5 by 0.5–1 µm, sometimes filamentous or coccobacillary. Most strains are motile with peritrichous flagella, and some are capsulated.

**MAYA:** The biochemical ID card?

**ALEX:** Aerobic, facultatively anaerobic. It grows across wide temperature and pH ranges. Then:
- catalase **+**, oxidase **–**;
- **Simmons citrate –**;
- **lactose +** (or prompt β-galactosidase);
- **indole +**;
- **urease –**;
- **lysine decarboxylase +**;
- **H₂S –**;
- no phenylalanine deaminase;
- does not liquefy gelatin;
- uses sodium acetate.

**MAYA:** So the short version is: lactose-positive, indole-positive, citrate-negative, lysine-positive.

**ALEX:** That's the one to memorize.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** There are four antigen types.

**O antigen.** 174 types have been described, O1 to O173, with 7 factors withdrawn, so the scheme has gaps. It's group-specific. Serogroups **O55 and O111** were involved in outbreaks of malignant diarrhea and neonatal meningitis.

**H antigen.** 53 type factors, H1 to H56 minus H13, H22 and H50. Those three were withdrawn because they belonged to *Citrobacter freundii*. Most cultures are weakly flagellated, so motility is boosted by repeated passages through 0.5% semisolid agar in **U-tubes**.

**K antigen.** 98 type-specific factors. They were originally split into three categories by heat resistance:
- **A**, polysaccharide, heat-stable at 100 °C;
- **B** and **L**, heat-labile, destroyed at 100 °C: fimbriae, outer membrane proteins, capsular polysaccharides.

The **K1 antigen** is a polymer of N-acetylneuraminic acid. It's poorly immunogenic and causes **neonatal meningitis**.

**F antigens (fimbrial):**
- F1 = type 1, the common fimbrial antigens;
- F2 = CFA I and F3 = CFA II, both ETEC in humans;
- F4 = K88, F5 = K99, F6 = K987, ETEC in pigs and cattle;
- F7–F12, human UTIs.

**MAYA:** How many antigenic types does that add up to?

**ALEX:** Over 1,000. The notation is O:K:H, O:K:H:F, or usually just O:H, as in O157:H7.

**MAYA:** And there's a warning.

**ALEX:** An important one. Placing a strain in an "O" serogroup is often approximate. Many strains belonging to "pathogenic" O groups show no pathogenic activity at all. That's why serotyping is done **only on strains already identified biochemically as *Escherichia***.

### Virulence factors

**MAYA:** This is the big section. Let's go slowly.

**ALEX:** There are three groups of pathogenic *E. coli*: diarrheagenic, uropathogenic and extraintestinal.

#### 1) The six diarrheagenic pathotypes

**ETEC (enterotoxigenic):**
- fimbrial colonization factors **CFA I and CFA II** (F2, F3), plus the **Longus** factor, which is characteristic of ETEC;
- enterotoxins: **LT I**, which is heat-labile, immunogenic, works via **cAMP** and is analogous to cholera toxin; and **STa**, which is heat-stable, non-immunogenic and works via **cGMP**;
- both toxins and the adherence factors are **plasmid-encoded**.

**EHEC / STEC / VTEC (enterohemorrhagic):**
- toxins **SLT I and SLT II** (Shiga-like toxins, verotoxins VT1/VT2), acquired by **lysogenic conversion**, meaning via a phage;
- SLT II is produced alone or together with SLT I in **97%** of strains;
- **intimin** (gene ***eaeA***), which produces **A/E lesions**;
- a virulence plasmid;
- over 200 VTEC serotypes, the most frequent being **O157:H7** and O157:H–. Sorbitol-positive serogroups include O26 and O111.

**EIEC (enteroinvasive):**
- the invasion mechanism is **identical to *Shigella***, plasmid-encoded, gene ***ipaH***;
- invasion has four phases: penetration of M cells; uptake by macrophages and release beneath the epithelium; intra- and intercellular multiplication; and cell death by blocked protein synthesis. The result is irreversible cytoplasmic lesions and ulcerations on an inflammatory background.

**EAggEC (enteroaggregative):**
- aggregative adherence fimbriae **AAF/I** (plasmid gene ***agg***);
- adherence in a **"stacked-brick"** pattern on HeLa cells, a tyrosine kinase-dependent process;
- enterotoxins **EALT**, heat-labile and antigenically related to *E. coli* hemolysin but without hemolytic activity, and **EAST 1**, heat-stable.

**EPEC (enteropathogenic):**
- the fimbrial adhesin **BFP** (bundle-forming pilus), with genes *bfpA* and *dspA* on the **EAF plasmid** (EPEC Adherence Factor). It produces adherent microcolonies;
- the chromosomal **LEE** locus with *eaeA* (intimin, "*E. coli* attaching and effacing"), *espB* (EPEC secretory protein) and *sep*;
- **A/E lesions** (attaching and effacing): tight adherence, formation of a "cup" or pedestal, cytoskeleton rearrangement and effacement of microvilli. Increased Ca²⁺ and tyrosine phosphorylation are involved.

**DAEC (diffusely adherent):**
- diffuse adherence to HeLa cells;
- chromosomally encoded afimbrial adhesins: **F1845, AfaD, AfaE**. AfaD promotes internalization of the bacterium.

**MAYA:** Six pathotypes: ETEC, EHEC, EIEC, EAggEC, EPEC, DAEC.

**ALEX:** That's the list.

#### 2) Uropathogenic *E. coli* (UPEC), over 80% of UTIs

**ALEX:**
- **Adhesins.** The **Pap fimbriae** ("pili associated with pyelonephritis"), also called **P pili**. Their receptor is the **P blood group antigen**, present on red cells, on uroepithelium and in urinary mucus. There are also afimbrial adhesins whose receptor is the **Dr blood group antigen**.
- **Hemolysin.** Plasmid-encoded, a calcium-dependent cytotoxin, common in upper UTIs.
- **Aerobactin.** An iron chelator, found in over 75% of strains from upper UTIs.
- **CNF I** (cytotoxic necrotizing factor I). A monomeric protein toxin. It causes dermonecrosis in the rabbit and multinucleated cells on HeLa, Vero and CHO cells, and it helps the bacterium spread both ways between urine and blood.
- **Frequent O antigens** in uropathogens: O1, O2, O4, O6, O7, O16, O18, O75. **K antigens**: K1, K2, K5, K13, K51. Nephritogenic strains produce more K antigen, which means more severe infection.

#### 3) ExPEC (extraintestinal pathogenic *E. coli*)

**ALEX:** Neonatal meningitis (NMEC, with the K1 antigen) and septicemias.

### Clinical significance

**MAYA:** Start with UTIs.

**ALEX:** *E. coli* is the most frequent cause, **60–90%** of cases. In the US, UTIs in premenopausal women mean 7 million consultations and a billion dollars a year in direct costs. Risk factors: urinary catheterization, poor hygiene, diabetes, immunodeficiency, malformations, obstructive uropathies, vesicoureteral reflux and pregnancy.

**MAYA:** Now the diarrheal diseases, the synoptic table of pathotypes. This is a classic exam table.

**ALEX:**

- **ETEC**: **"travelers' diarrhea."** Watery, cholera-like diarrhea, cramps, acute dehydration. Site: **small intestine**. At-risk groups: **children under 2** in developing countries, and adult travelers. Serogroups: O6, O8, O11, O15, O20, O25, O27, O63, O78, O80, O85, O115, O128, O148, O153, O159, O167.

- **EHEC**: watery diarrhea progressing to **hemorrhagic colitis**, bloody stools, cramps, fever, and **hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)**, meaning hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and kidney failure, in about **7% of children**. Site: **colon**. Risk ages: **over 1 year and the elderly**. Source: **undercooked meat**. Serogroups: O157, O26, O111, O113, O128, O145.

- **EIEC**: **dysentery-like syndrome**, mucoid bloody stools, fever, cramps. Site: **colon**. Adults and travelers. Serogroups: O28, O29, O112, O124, O136, O143, O144, O152, O164, O167, O173.

- **EAggEC**: **persistent watery diarrhea**, acute and chronic, with vomiting. Site: small intestine. Children. Serogroups: O3, O15, O44, O77, O78, O86, O91, O92, O111, O113, O126, O141, O146.

- **EPEC**: persistent watery diarrhea, fever, vomiting, mucoid stools. This is the **"malignant diarrhea of the newborn,"** a toxic-septic syndrome. There were epidemics in Europe and North America from 1940 to 1960, and today it's a leading cause of infant diarrhea in developing countries. Risk age around **6 months**. Serogroups: O26, O55, O86, O111, O114, O119, O125, O126, O127, O128, O142, O158.

- **DAEC**: persistent watery diarrhea. Site: colon. Age **2–6 years**. Serogroups (annex): O11, O15, O18, O21, O75, O83, O128.

**MAYA:** And beyond diarrhea and UTIs?

**ALEX:** Neonatal meningitis (K1), septicemia, wound infections, nosocomial pneumonia, biliary infections and peritonitis.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

#### 1. Collection and transport

**ALEX:** It depends on the infection.

- **Digestive infections**: diarrheal stool, possibly hemorrhagic and containing shreds of mucosa; vomit; the incriminated food. Collect early and before antibiotics, in **Cary-Blair**.
- **UTIs**: **midstream urine, first morning void, after local cleansing**. Transport within **30 minutes maximum, otherwise at +4 °C**. Missing that window compromises both diagnosis and treatment. Alternatives are **suprapubic puncture** or catheterization.
- **Other**: blood cultures, CSF, pus, bronchial secretions.

#### 2. Macroscopic and microscopic examination

**ALEX:** Macroscopically: watery or hemorrhagic stool with mucosal shreds (EHEC), or cloudy urine.

Microscopically, stool gets a wet mount between slide and coverslip. You're looking for red cells and PMNs, which point to EHEC or EIEC.

For urine, a wet mount of the centrifuged sediment shows leukocytes, red cells, casts and bacteria. Two numbers to remember:
- **≥1 leukocyte per oil-immersion field** suggests pyuria;
- if, from homogenized urine sampled with a **0.01 ml calibrated loop**, you see **1–2 bacteria per oil-immersion field**, that suggests **≥10⁵ CFU/ml**.

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:**
- Weakly and moderately selective media: MacConkey, EMB/Levine, ADCL, plus blood agar.
- **For EHEC O157: sorbitol MacConkey (SMAC).** *E. coli* O157:H7 does **not** ferment sorbitol, or only late. Suspect colonies are **colorless**, 1–3 mm, type S. Sorbitol-positive colonies are pink. **Careful**: sorbitol-positive EHEC strains exist too, for example O26 and O111.
- **Quantitative urine culture** with a calibrated loop:
  - **≥10⁵ CFU/ml** = urinary infection;
  - **10⁴–10⁵ CFU/ml** with leukocyturia and suggestive symptoms = positive;
  - **any count from a suprapubic puncture** = positive.
- *Escherichia* grows well on weakly selective media, is partly inhibited on moderately selective media, and does **not** grow on highly selective media.

#### 4. Identification

**4a. Morphology and staining.** Gram-negative rods, 2–5 by 0.5–1 µm, motile (except the "inactive" type), sometimes capsulated.

**4b. Culture characters.** S colonies are convex, moist, shiny and smooth-edged, and they emulsify easily. R colonies are dry, crenated and hard to emulsify. M colonies are mucoid. They're lactose-positive: **red on MacConkey, with a green metallic sheen on EMB/Levine**.

**MAYA:** And hemolysis?

**ALEX:** On rabbit blood agar some strains show incomplete hemolysis. It has **nothing to do with enteropathogenicity**, but hemolytic strains are isolated more often from upper UTIs.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + with gas, lactose/sucrose +, H₂S –.
- MIU/MILF: motile +, indole +, urease –, lysine decarboxylase +, PAD –.
- Simmons citrate –.
- **EHEC O157:H7 is sorbitol-negative**, while most other strains are sorbitol-positive.

**MAYA:** And the *Escherichia/Shigella* table, Buiuc 31-8.

**ALEX:** The percentages of positive strains for normal *E. coli*: indole 98, lysine decarboxylase 90, ornithine decarboxylase 65, gas from glucose 95, lactose 95, sucrose 50, mannitol 98, adonitol 5, sorbitol 94, cellobiose 2, acetate 90, motility 95, ONPG 95. In the "inactive" *E. coli* the values drop sharply: lactose 25, motility 5, gas 5.

**MAYA:** Then galleries.

**ALEX:** API 10 S, API 20 E, API Rapid 20 E, and the automated systems.

**4d. Antigenic characters.**
- Slide agglutination or latex agglutination with polyvalent and monovalent sera. For EHEC there are commercial sera: O157, H7, and total O157:H7.
- A large amount of K antigen can **mask O**, so suspensions are **heated for 1 hour at 100 °C** before O agglutination.
- For EPEC: **OB sera**, which contain both anti-O and anti-B agglutinins. For EIEC: agglutination or latex with polyvalent and monovalent sera O28, O29, O124, O136, O143, O144, O152, O164, O167.
- If agglutination is negative on a biochemically suggestive strain, keep a dense suspension at 100 °C for 30–60 minutes and repeat.

**4e. Pathogenicity.**
- **EHEC**: lethal hemorrhagic enterocolitis in the piglet; cytopathic effect on **Vero** cells (A/E lesions, adherence, effacement of microvilli).
- **EIEC**: **Sereny test**, inoculating about 10⁹ CFU into the guinea pig's conjunctival sac. Purulent keratoconjunctivitis follows in 1–3 days.
- **ETEC**: ligated rabbit ileal loop, or intragastric inoculation of the newborn mouse.
- **EPEC**: diffuse adherence and A/E lesions on Hep-2/HeLa. **EAggEC**: "brick" aggregation on Hep-2, with invasion shown on HeLa. **DAEC**: diffuse adherence on HeLa and Caco-2.

**4f. Virulence.**
- Toxins: ELISA, latex, VET-RPLA and immunodiffusion (the Biken test) for **LT I**; ELISA for **STa**; seroneutralization of the cytotoxic effect on Vero cells for **SLT 1 and SLT 2**.
- PCR: *elt* (LT I) and *est* (STa) for **ETEC**; *stx1/stx2* and *eaeA* for **EHEC**; *ipaH* for **EIEC**; the EAggEC plasmid for **EAggEC**; the *afa* operon and *eaeA* for **EPEC**; the *afa* gene cluster for **DAEC**.

**4g. Phage typing.**
- Lysotyping schemes were developed for the EPEC serogroups **O26, O55, O111**, widely used when those serogroups dominated epidemiologically in Europe.
- Phage typing of **O157** uses a stable scheme, with **phage types 2 and 49** epidemically dominant.

**4h. Other typing.** Biotyping, colicin typing, resistotyping, and plasmid profiling, with plasmid profiling giving clearly better results than resistotyping. Full serotyping, covering 166 O factors, is possible only in reference labs. Routine labs use three sets of sera for the O serogroups involved in pathotypes.

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- **RFLP, MLST, ribotyping**. The course explicitly mentions these for EHEC, including for epidemiology. PCR or nucleotide probes for pathogenicity genes, directly from the specimen or from SMAC colonies.
- *Modern layer*: **MALDI-TOF**, which still can't separate *E. coli* from *Shigella*; **multiplex PCR panels from stool** (*stx1/stx2, eae, ipaH*, LT/ST, *aggR*); and **WGS + cgMLST**, now the standard for STEC, with *in silico* serotyping and outbreak investigation (PulseNet, ECDC).

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- Disk diffusion, standardized. **In acute diarrhea**, an antibiogram is usually done only for resistance surveillance. If treatment is decided on, it should preferably be antibiogram-guided. **In UTIs and systemic infections it's mandatory.**
- ***E. coli* has no notable natural resistance.** The wild type is susceptible to aminopenicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and carbapenems. So **any resistance is acquired**.
- Watch for: **ESBLs**, mainly **CTX-M-15 in the pandemic clone ST131**; plasmid AmpC (**CMY-2**); carbapenemases (**OXA-48, NDM, KPC**); fluoroquinolone resistance (*gyrA/parC* mutations, *qnr*); and colistin resistance (***mcr-1***).

**MAYA:** And the critical clinical point.

**ALEX:** **In STEC/EHEC, antibiotics are contraindicated.** They raise the risk of HUS through massive toxin release. Quinolones and cotrimoxazole induce the lambdoid phage that carries the toxin genes.

### Serological diagnosis

**ALEX:** **Not done.** There is a humoral response, but it has no significance for protection or for diagnosis.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Acute diarrhea**: usually no antibiotics. Fluid and electrolyte rebalancing is strictly necessary.
- **UTIs and systemic infections**: according to the antibiogram and clinical context.
- **Prevention**: hygiene in health facilities (neonatology, maternity, urology) and in public catering; proper cooking of semi-prepared meat products; careful use of catheters. The document has a memorable line here: **"any catheter kept in place for more than two weeks is 'certainly infected.'"** Urinary antiseptics are used for recurrent infections.
- **Surveillance**: ECDC recommends surveillance systems for VTEC. Romania has **no national data** on O157:H7 and HUS.

## PART A — *KLEBSIELLA*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The type species was described in 1885 as *Bakterium lactis aerogenes*, and the current name dates from 1954. The genus has 10 species. The medically important ones are ***K. pneumoniae*, *K. oxytoca*, *K. ozaenae* and *K. rhinoscleromatis***.

**MAYA:** And the modern changes?

**ALEX:** *K. ozaenae* and *K. rhinoscleromatis* are now **subspecies of *K. pneumoniae***: subsp. *ozaenae* and subsp. *rhinoscleromatis*. ***Enterobacter aerogenes* has been reclassified as *Klebsiella aerogenes***. The *K. pneumoniae* complex also includes *K. variicola* and *K. quasipneumoniae*. And some strains have moved to the genus ***Raoultella***.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** It's widespread in nature, in soil and water, and also in human and animal intestines and the upper respiratory flora. In hospitals, **multiresistant strains are selected** that colonize the urinary or respiratory tract in adults and the intestinal tract in children, especially after prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Capsulated** Gram-negative rods. The capsule is **2–3 times wider than the bacterium itself**.
- **Non-motile**, non-sporulating, sometimes arranged in pairs.
- Intense carbohydrate metabolism, producing acid and **large amounts of gas**, except *K. rhinoscleromatis*. They ferment most sugars, **except dulcitol**.
- They produce acetoin (**VP +**), **lysine decarboxylase** and **urease**. They grow on **Simmons citrate**. **No H₂S, no phenylalanine deaminase.**

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- **O somatic antigens** (LPS), group-specific.
- **K capsular antigens**, polysaccharide and type-specific. There are **over 80 types**, identified by the **capsular swelling reaction**, agglutination, coagglutination or latex agglutination. The Kauffmann and Ørskov scheme has 77 serovars. Because there are so many serovars and they're antigenically related, typing is laborious.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **The capsule (K antigen) is the main pathogenicity factor**. It gives resistance to phagocytosis.
- **Endotoxin** (the O antigen), released when the bacterium is destroyed.
- It's conditionally pathogenic and acts through **multiplication and invasiveness**.
- **Type 1 and type 3 fimbriae**, for adherence to uroepithelium and catheters and for biofilm formation. Siderophores: enterobactin, yersiniabactin, aerobactin, salmochelin.

**MAYA:** And the hypervirulent strains.

**ALEX:** That's the *modern layer*. **Hypervirulent *K. pneumoniae* (hvKp)** is often K1 or K2, with genes *rmpA/rmpA2* and *magA*, and a **hypermucoviscous** phenotype. The **"string test"** is positive when touching a colony with a loop pulls a filament longer than **5 mm**. These strains cause cryptogenic pyogenic **liver abscesses**, **endophthalmitis** and **meningitis**, in people **without immunosuppression**, especially in Southeast Asia.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- **Pneumonia.** Only **1–3% of pneumonias**, but **extremely severe**, necrotizing and hemorrhagic, especially in people with poor defenses: the elderly, chronic lung disease, diabetics and alcoholics. The sputum is mucoid, dark red, **"redcurrant jelly."**
- **Other infections**: urinary infections, enteritis (especially in children), food poisoning, wound and postoperative infections, and localized or generalized septic complications. ***Klebsiella* septicemia has a severe prognosis.**
- *K. pneumoniae* and *K. oxytoca* are among the most frequent enterobacteria in **nosocomial infections**.
- ***K. ozaenae*** is isolated from the nasal mucosa of patients with **ozena**, a progressive atrophy of the mucosa with loss of smell. ***K. rhinoscleromatis*** causes **rhinoscleroma**, a destructive granuloma of the nose and pharynx. Both are **exceptionally rare** in ENT practice in Romania.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.** Depends on site: sputum for pneumonia, urine, feces, blood, pus, bronchial secretions, tracheal aspirate. General rules apply.

**2. Macro and micro.**
- Macroscopically: mucoid, dark red **"redcurrant jelly" sputum**.
- Microscopically: **at least two sputum smears**, one stained with **methylene blue** and one with **Gram**, examined under oil immersion. Note the alveolar cells (macrophages), inflammatory cells (leukocytes), and relatively large Gram-negative rods with a big capsule. **With methylene blue, the capsule shows up as a halo** around the bacteria.
- The microscopy has to show that the specimen is acceptable (sputum validation criteria) and give a presumptive identification.
- Rapid direct identification on the specimen is possible with the **capsular swelling reaction** using anti-capsular antibodies.

**3. Culture.**
- It grows easily on routine media in 18–24 h at 35–37 °C. **Weakly selective** media like MacConkey are preferred. It **does not grow on highly selective media** and is partly inhibited on moderately selective ones (ADCL, SS, XLD), where colonies are lactose/xylose-positive at 48 h and less mucoid.
- On lactose media: **lactose-positive, large** colonies (2–3 mm at 24 h, over 4 mm at 48 h), domed, viscous and creamy. They look **"like a drop of honey"** and **"flow"** across the surface of the medium, tending to merge.
- Inoculation into susceptible lab animals, the white mouse, is also possible.

**4a. Morphology.** Large, capsulated Gram-negative rods, non-motile, single, in pairs or short chains. The capsule can be lost in culture.

**4b. Culture.** Lactose-positive, M-type colonies, large, domed, viscous, "drop of honey," confluent.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + with **abundant gas**, lactose/sucrose + (fermented quickly), H₂S –.
- MIU: **non-motile**, **indole –**, urease +. The exception is ***K. oxytoca*, which is indole-positive**. That's the unique trait that separates it from *K. pneumoniae*.
- Citrate +, VP +, lysine decarboxylase +, PAD –.

**MAYA:** There's a three-trait combination that separates *Klebsiella* from its neighbors.

**ALEX:** **Non-motile + lysine decarboxylase-positive + no phenylalanine deaminase** separates *Klebsiella* from *Enterobacter*, *Hafnia* and *Serratia*.

## PART B — *ENTEROBACTER*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The genus has 16 species. Only two are frequent in hospital infections, ***E. cloacae* and *E. aerogenes***, and 7 others have been isolated occasionally from human disease. The species: *E. cloacae, E. aerogenes, E. agglomerans, E. gergoviae, E. sakazakii, E. taylorae, E. amnigenus, E. asburiae, E. hormaechei*.

**MAYA:** And there's a lot of renaming.

**ALEX:** A lot. *E. aerogenes* → ***Klebsiella aerogenes***. *E. sakazakii* → ***Cronobacter sakazakii***. *E. agglomerans* → ***Pantoea agglomerans***. And *E. cloacae* is really a **complex of species**: *E. cloacae, E. hormaechei, E. asburiae, E. kobei, E. ludwigii, E. roggenkampii*. These can only be separated reliably by molecular methods (*hsp60*) or WGS.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** Environment (soil, water, plants), and the intestinal microbiota of humans and animals. They're part of the biological recycling ecosystems. They're also an important **hospital reservoir**: infusion fluids, parenteral nutrition, equipment.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Motile** (peritrichous) enterobacteria.
- They ferment glucose with **large amounts of gas**, and ferment lactose (β-galactosidase +), rhamnose and sorbitol.
- They produce acetylmethylcarbinol (**VP +**) and **ornithine decarboxylase**.
- **No indole, no H₂S, no PAD.**
- Exception: some biovars of *E. agglomerans* produce no gas.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** A serotyping scheme with **60 O factors and 34 H factors** was developed, but it's used only on a limited basis. A phage typing scheme was **abandoned for lack of sensitivity**, because over 20% of strains were untypeable.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- Conditionally pathogenic; endotoxin; can colonize medical materials.
- **The clinically essential factor is inducible chromosomal AmpC β-lactamase.** Treatment with third-generation cephalosporins selects **derepressed mutants**, so you get treatment failure **during the course of therapy**.
- ***Cronobacter sakazakii*** survives in **dehydrated powdered infant formula** and resists osmotic stress and drying.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- Hospital infections: neonatal sepsis, meningitis, septicemia, UTIs, ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter infections and wound infections. In nosocomial musculoskeletal and skin infections, *Enterobacter* ranks just after staphylococci and pseudomonads.
- ***Cronobacter sakazakii***: **neonatal meningitis and necrotizing enterocolitis**, linked to **powdered milk formula**, with **40–80% mortality**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.** Blood, CSF, urine, tracheal aspirate, pus, catheters. General rules apply.

**2. Microscopy.** Gram-negative rods with no distinctive features.

**3. Culture.**
- On simple media all species grow quickly, forming **large 2–3 mm round, typical S colonies** in 24 h at 37 °C. Some *E. agglomerans* strains form rough, **"cauliflower-like"** colonies.
- ***E. sakazakii* and *E. agglomerans* consistently produce a characteristic yellow pigment in 2–4 days.**
- On weakly selective media: large lactose-positive colonies. *E. aerogenes* can form large lactose-positive colonies that **look like *Klebsiella***. In principle the genus doesn't form colonies on moderately selective media.

**4a.** Motile, non-sporulating, **non-capsulated** Gram-negative rods.

**4b.** Large S colonies; yellow pigment (*E. sakazakii, E. agglomerans*); lactose-positive.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + with gas, lactose/sucrose +, H₂S –.
- MILF: motile, indole –, **lysine decarboxylase + only in *E. aerogenes* and *E. gergoviae***, PAD –.
- VP +, ODC +, citrate +.

**MAYA:** Species differentiation, Buiuc 31-22.

**ALEX:** Percent positive:
- ***E. aerogenes***: LDC 98, ADH 0, ODC 98, sucrose 100, sorbitol 100, adonitol 98, raffinose 96, malonate 95, urease 2.
- ***E. cloacae***: LDC 0, **ADH 97**, ODC 96, sorbitol 95, adonitol 25, urease 65.
- ***E. agglomerans***: LDC 0, ADH 0, ODC 0, **yellow pigment**.
- ***E. gergoviae***: LDC 90, ODC 100, **urease 93**.
- ***E. sakazakii***: ADH 99, ODC 91, **sorbitol 0**, raffinose 99, **yellow pigment**.

Definitive identification is **primarily exoenzymatic**, on extended test sets (Table 31-5). Preliminary assignment is done on TSI-MILF.

**4d.** Serotyping (60 O, 34 H), limited use, specialized labs only.
**4e.** Pathogenicity tests not done routinely.
**4f.** *Modern*: PCR for ***hsp60*** to assign strains to clusters of the *E. cloacae* complex; detection of AmpC genes.
**4g.** Phage typing **abandoned** (over 20% untypeable).
**4h.** **Plasmid spectrum** is one of the typing methods most used to investigate hospital infections caused by *E. aerogenes* and *E. cloacae*. Other genetic methods are used sporadically. **Resistotyping** can be useful, since species vary in susceptibility to floxacins, third-generation cephalosporins and aminoglycosides.
**4i.** MALDI-TOF (with limits inside the *E. cloacae* complex); WGS/cgMLST for outbreaks; *hsp60/rpoB* sequencing.

**5. Susceptibility.**
- Standardized disk diffusion; MIC when needed.
- **Natural resistance**: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first-generation cephalosporins, cefoxitin (inducible AmpC). **Cefuroxime is also inactive.**
- **Don't report "susceptible" to third-generation cephalosporins without a warning**, because of the risk of AmpC derepression during treatment. **Cefepime or carbapenems are preferred in severe infections.**
- ESBLs and carbapenemases are increasingly frequent: VIM, NDM, OXA-48, KPC.

### Serology
**ALEX:** Not done.

### Treatment
**ALEX:** According to the antibiogram. In severe infections, carbapenems or cefepime. **Avoid third-generation cephalosporins as monotherapy.**

## PART C — *SERRATIA*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The genus includes 12 species, 10 of which have been isolated from human infections. The main species is ***S. marcescens***. Others: *S. liquefaciens, S. rubidaea, S. odorifera, S. plymuthica, S. ficaria, S. fonticola*.

**MAYA:** And it's ancient.

**ALEX:** *S. marcescens*, with its characteristic red pigment, was **described in 1823**. Its presence was being discussed about **three centuries BC**. Today the genus belongs to the family ***Yersiniaceae***.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** Environment (water, soil, plants) and biological recycling ecosystems. In hospitals it turns up in solutions, equipment and **diluted disinfectants**.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Motile** enterobacteria that grow luxuriantly on simple and weakly or moderately selective media.
- Metabolically:
  - **small amounts of gas** from glucose; *S. marcescens* is often **anaerogenic**;
  - produce **lysine and ornithine decarboxylases**, **DNase**, **proteases** and **lipases**;
  - **ferment lactose late**, but are **promptly β-galactosidase-positive**;
  - **no indole, no H₂S, no PAD**;
  - **VP +**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** The scheme proposed by **Ewing and Davis** and extended by **Grimont** identifies **over 70 serovars** by O and H agglutination. One unique detail: unlike other enterobacteria, *Serratia* **H antigens are prepared with 6‰ phenol**, not with formalin.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:** Conditionally pathogenic, with a rich **lipolytic and proteolytic equipment**, **high antibiotic resistance** (especially to cephalosporins), and endotoxin. *S. marcescens* is a recognized urinary and respiratory pathogen and causes serious primary or secondary bacteremia and septicemia.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:** A relatively frequent conditional pathogen in hospital infections: urinary, respiratory, bacteremia, septicemia, catheter infections, **endophthalmitis** and wound infections. Together with *E. coli*, it ranks just after staphylococci and pseudomonads in nosocomial musculoskeletal and skin infections. It's **almost exclusively extraintestinal**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.** Urine, blood, bronchial aspirate, pus, catheters, and **suspect infusion fluids** (outbreak investigation).

**2.** Gram-negative rods on the smear. Macroscopically, rarely, red pigmentation of the specimen or dressings.

**3. Culture.**
- On agar, medium-sized, mostly S colonies in 24 h at 37 °C. **At 48–72 h some cultures of *S. marcescens*, *S. rubidaea* and *S. plymuthica* produce a bright red pigment, prodigiosin, which is unmistakable.**
- On blood agar *S. marcescens* may give hemolysis over a **restricted** area.
- On selective media: **lactose-negative colonies that stay that way even after several days**.
- On egg yolk media: **lecithinase-positive** colonies.

**4a.** Motile Gram-negative rods.
**4b.** Medium S colonies, lactose-negative on selective media, red pigment at 48–72 h, restricted hemolysis, lecithinase +.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + (gas absent or minimal), lactose/sucrose – (late +), H₂S –.
- MILF: motile +, indole –, LDC +, PAD –.
- VP +, **DNase +, gelatinase +**, ONPG +.

**MAYA:** And there's an exam trap with *Salmonella* Typhi.

**ALEX:** A classic one. On TSI/MILF, *Serratia* and ***S.* Typhi look nearly identical**. Both produce no gas or very little. H₂S is absent, although *S.* Typhi may show a black dot at the stab. Neither ferments lactose or sucrose. Both are motile and indole-negative, both are lysine decarboxylase-positive and PAD-negative. **You settle it with ornithine decarboxylase, DNase, gelatinase, VP, and fermentation of adonitol, sorbitol and L-arabinose.**

**MAYA:** Species differentiation, Buiuc 31-23.

**ALEX:**
- ***S. marcescens***: LDC 99, ODC 99, gas 55, adonitol 40, **L-arabinose 0**, D-sorbitol 99, malonate 3, red pigment 30.
- ***S. liquefaciens***: L-arabinose 98, sorbitol 95.
- ***S. rubidaea***: ODC 0, adonitol 99, arabinose 100, malonate 94, pigment 50.
- ***S. odorifera***: LDC/ODC 100, arabinose 100. Its characteristic feature is a **putrid smell**.
- ***S. plymuthica***: LDC/ODC 0, pigment 20.

Definitive identification uses extended sets. The essentials are **lysine and ornithine decarboxylases, gas from glucose, and fermentation of adonitol, sorbitol and L-arabinose**.

**4d. Antigens.** Serological typing by O and H agglutination (Ewing-Davis extended by Grimont, over 70 serovars). This is the **preferred** method and widely available.
**4e.** Not done routinely.
**4f.** PCR for resistance genes; DNase, lipase and protease production as phenotypic markers.
**4g. Phage typing.** Schemes were proposed independently by **Farmer et al. (1995)** and **Traub et al. (1991)**, but they never spread widely. Serotyping is preferred.
**4h.** Bacteriocin typing. **Resistotyping** has important limits in this genus, because *S. marcescens* acquires multiple resistance factors so easily. Resistance to aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin) and some cephalosporins can be relevant for hospital-circulating resistotypes. Molecular typing analyzes resistance plasmids.
**4i.** MALDI-TOF; PFGE and WGS/cgMLST for nosocomial outbreaks, which are **frequent in neonatology**.

**5. Susceptibility.**
- **Natural resistance**: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first-generation cephalosporins, cefoxitin, cefuroxime (inducible AmpC), **colistin/polymyxins** and nitrofurantoin.
- Frequent acquired resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (AmpC derepression), ESBLs and carbapenemases (**SME**, KPC, NDM).

**ALEX:** No serology. Treat according to the antibiogram, and **don't use colistin**.

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The genus has 12 species. Buiuc Table 31-20 differentiates the 8 medically important ones: ***C. freundii*, *C. diversus* (= *C. koseri*)**, *C. amalonaticus, C. farmeri, C. youngae, C. braakii, C. werkmanii, C. sedlakii*. The current name of *C. diversus* is ***Citrobacter koseri***.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** Human and animal intestinal microbiota, and the environment. In feces **it can be confused with *Salmonella***.

**ALEX:** **Motile** enterobacteria that ferment glucose **with gas**, lactose (**slowly**), dulcitol and cellobiose. Some species are urease-positive.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**

- *C. diversus* has a scheme of 14 O factors and 7 H factors.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:** Conditionally pathogenic. Enteric strains have intense **cytotoxic activity**. Endotoxin. ***C. koseri* has a particular tropism for the CNS in newborns**: it survives inside macrophages and forms **brain abscesses**.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- Most species have been reported in human disease, especially in immunosuppressed people.

It **can agglutinate with the polyvalent "O" and Vi anti-*Salmonella* sera**.
- ***C. diversus (koseri)***: septicemia, **neonatal meningitis**, UTIs, and less often enterocolitis. It's hard to distinguish from *E. coli*, especially in urinary isolates.

**1.** Feces, urine, blood, CSF (neonatology), bronchial aspirate.
**2.** Nonspecific; Gram-negative rods.
**3. Culture.** Culture behavior is shared with *Salmonella* and *Proteus*: it **behaves as a lactose-negative enterobacterium on selective media**. **On XLD it forms yellow colonies resembling *Proteus*.**
**4a.** Motile Gram-negative rods.
**4b.** S colonies, lactose-negative on selective media (slow lactose fermentation); yellow on XLD.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI + MILF separate the genus satisfactorily among lactose-negative groups. **Colonies that ferment glucose with gas, produce H₂S or indole, lack LDC and PAD, and are motile very probably belong to *Citrobacter*.**
- An 8-test set characterizes the species (Buiuc 31-20, % positive):

  - ***C. amalonaticus***: indole 100, ODC 95, malonate 1;
  - ***C. werkmanii***: indole 0, H₂S 100, malonate 100.

## PART A — *PROTEUS*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The genus has **8 species**, and only **3 are medically important**: ***P. vulgaris*, *P. mirabilis* and *P. penneri***. Others include *P. myxofaciens* and *P. hauseri*.

**MAYA:** And two former members moved out.

**ALEX:** ***P. morganii* became *Morganella morganii***, and ***P. rettgeri* became *Providencia rettgeri***. Today they all sit in the family ***Morganellaceae*** within the order *Enterobacterales*.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** It's ubiquitous: **putrefying organic matter**, food, soil and water. It's part of the human intestinal flora, and *P. vulgaris* is routinely isolated from stool as part of the normal microbiota.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- Gram-negative rods with marked **polymorphism**. The name comes from ancient mythology, after **Proteus, the shape-shifting sea god**. On a smear you can see rods, coccobacilli and filaments of **30–80 µm**.
- **Very motile**, with many peritrichous flagella (non-flagellated strains also exist). Non-capsulated, non-sporulating.
- Metabolically:
  - ferment glucose **with gas**;
  - produce **H₂S**, **urease** and **phenylalanine deaminase**;
  - **don't ferment lactose**;
  - **don't produce lysine decarboxylase or β-galactosidase**.
- Aerobic, facultatively anaerobic, tolerant of large swings in temperature and pH.
- In liquid peptone media they give off a characteristic **putrefaction odor**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- **O antigen** (LPS, endotoxin), group-specific. **H antigen** (flagellar, protein), shared by several types.
- An antigenic scheme by **Kauffmann and Perch**, modeled on the *Salmonella* Kauffmann-White scheme, with **55 O factors and 31 H factors**. Serovars proved to be good epidemiological markers, especially in **urology units** where cross-infection is common. Serotyping was advocated as early as **1917 by Weil and Felix**.
- Strains **OX-19, OX-2 and OX-K** (Vinkle scheme) share antigens with **rickettsiae**. They're used as antigens in the **Weil-Felix agglutination reaction**, for serodiagnosis of primary rickettsial infections.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- Pathogenic through **multiplication and invasiveness**, under special conditions: leaving its natural habitat, or a host with immune deficiencies.
- **Endotoxin**, released when the cell wall is destroyed.
- **Exceptional motility.** It allows **ascending invasion of the urinary tract** and **"swarming" over catheter surfaces**.
- **Urease.** It **alkalinizes urine** and promotes **stone formation, struvite stones**. Stones then promote further UTIs or make them chronic, and crystals plus biofilm protect bacteria from antibiotics.
- **Remarkable resistance to antibiotics and chemotherapeutics.** It's also relatively resistant in the environment, in food, in antiseptic solutions and in detergents, which is a hospital problem. It **survives for months at low temperatures, including in frozen food**.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- Mainly **outside the digestive tract**. ***P. mirabilis* is, after *E. coli*, the most frequent cause of urinary infections.** *P. vulgaris* has also been implicated in **gastroenteritis resembling food poisoning**.
- Accidentally: localized infections, especially **abdominal surgical wounds**, genital infections, pleurisy, otitis, septicemia, metastatic sites during bacteremia, and nosocomial infections.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.**
- **Urine** (most often), pus, purulent exudates, feces, vomit, food, blood.
- Urine: transport **within 30 minutes, otherwise "cold" at +4 °C**. Macroscopically it may be cloudy or purulent.

**2. Macro and micro.**
- Macroscopically: cloudy or purulent urine.
- Microscopically: a wet mount of the centrifuged urine sediment shows **large numbers of very motile bacteria**, leukocytes and red cells. A stained smear from homogenized urine can also be made: Gram-negative rods with marked polymorphism (rods, coccobacilli, filaments up to 30 µm), non-capsulated, non-sporulating.
- Same thresholds as for *E. coli*: **≥1 leukocyte per oil field** suggests pyuria, and **1–2 bacteria per field** with a 0.01 ml calibrated loop suggests **≥10⁵ CFU/ml**.

**3. Culture.**
- It grows on simple media, including liquid peptone media, with a putrid smell.
- **Quantitative urine culture is mandatory** (≥10⁵ CFU/ml).

**MAYA:** And the problem on plain plates.

**ALEX:** On simple media, blood agar and AABTL **you can't get isolated colonies, because of swarming (invasion or migration)**. That spreading growth itself suggests *Proteus*.

**MAYA:** How do you stop it?

**ALEX:** Swarming is **inhibited by bile salts, sodium thiosulfate, bismuth sulfite, sulfonamides, neomycin, activated charcoal and acids**. In other words, it's inhibited on the **selective-differential media** (MacConkey, ADCL), where you get **isolated, lactose-negative S colonies**. And there's a catch: on selective media, *Proteus* behaves as a lactose-negative group, and **its colonies can't be distinguished from *Salmonella* or *Shigella***.

**4a. Morphology.** Gram-negative rods, highly polymorphic (rods, coccobacilli, filaments of 30–80 µm), very motile, non-capsulated, non-sporulating.

**4b. Culture: three characteristic phenomena with identification value.**

**ALEX:** This is a favorite exam topic.

1. **Swarming (invasion or migration).** Inoculate the edge of a plate of **simple 2% agar with no inhibitors**. *Proteus* grows in **successive concentric waves**, covering the whole surface with a continuous sheet of growth. It's characteristic of the genus.

2. **The "demarcation line" phenomenon (Dienes phenomenon).** Inoculate **two different *Proteus* strains** at opposite points of the same plate. The waves **stop 1–2 mm apart**, leaving a **demarcation line** between them. If the strains are **identical**, the waves **merge** into one continuous sheet. It's used as an **epidemic marker**.

3. **The "climbing" phenomenon.** Inoculate the specimen into the **condensation water of a slant tube** and incubate it **upright**. In 24 hours you get a **pure culture of *Proteus***, grown in **stacked waves** up to the top of the medium. It's a **method of isolation in pure culture**.

Plus, on selective media: isolated lactose-negative S colonies.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + (sometimes with gas), lactose –, **H₂S +**.
- MIU/MILF: motile +, **urease +**, **PAD +** (the key character for the group), LDC –.
- Simmons citrate variable (d). β-galactosidase –.

**MAYA:** What separates *Proteus* from the other lactose-negatives?

**ALEX:** **PAD production plus absence of lysine decarboxylase.** And **indole production by *P. vulgaris* separates the *Proteus* species at the preliminary stage.**

**MAYA:** Species differentiation. There's a correction in the document.

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** Some history first. The antigenic structure was originally, and **wrongly**, taken to define the species. That produced a genus with **over 2,000 "species."**

**MAYA:** Then Ewing.

**ALEX:** **Ewing's classification had 3 species**:
- *S. typhi*, pathogenic only for humans;
- *S. choleraesuis* (later *S. enterica*), pathogenic in pigs and occasionally humans, with 1,416 serotypes;
- *S. enteritidis*, causing diarrhea in humans and animals.

**MAYA:** And today?

**ALEX:** **The current classification (Popoff and Le Minor) has 2 species:**
- ***S. enterica***, with **6 subspecies**: *enterica, salamae, arizonae, diarizonae, houtenae, indica*;
- ***S. bongori***.

Serovars keep the names given by the Kauffmann-White scheme, **written in roman type, not italics**: *Salmonella* serovar Typhimurium, *Salmonella* ser. Enteritidis, or simply Typhimurium.

**MAYA:** If you counted serotypes as species?

**ALEX:** You'd have **over 2,390 "species."** *S. enterica* subsp. *enterica* contains the serovars found mainly in **humans and mammals**. The other subspecies and *S. bongori* are found in **cold-blooded animals**.

**MAYA:** And the DNA homology story.

**ALEX:** *Salmonella* and *E. coli* share about **90% DNA–DNA homology**, so they ought to be in the same genus. **Protests from microbiologists and clinicians used to the old names** prevented that.

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- Very widespread in nature, present in **all animal species**: birds, fish, snakes, insects.
- **For *S.* Typhi, humans are the only reservoir**, and the same goes for Paratyphi. With few exceptions (Typhi and Paratyphi in humans, Abortus ovis in sheep, Typhisuis in pigs, Gallinarum/Pullorum in poultry), the other serovars have **no host specificity**.
- Two major sources pollute soil, waste water and surface water: **humans and animals**. They survive in the environment for months or even years. Multiplication in the environment is possible but insignificant.
- **The food link is essential** in human infection: meat, **eggs, especially duck eggs**, milk, and contaminated animal feed meal.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- Gram-negative rods, **2–4 by 0.4–0.6 µm**, motile with **peritrichous flagella**. The exception is serovars **Gallinarum/Pullorum, which are non-motile**. Non-capsulated, non-sporulating.
- They **ferment glucose with gas** (**not *S.* Typhi**), produce acid from **mannitol and sorbitol**, and produce **lysine decarboxylase, arginine hydrolase, ornithine decarboxylase and H₂S** (with some exceptions). They use **citrate as the sole carbon source**.
- **No indole, urease, PAD or acetylmethylcarbinol (VP –)**. They don't degrade adonitol or esculin. **They don't ferment lactose.**

**MAYA:** And survival numbers.

**ALEX:** They multiply between **7 and 48 °C, at pH 4–8**. They're **not very heat-resistant**, killed in **5–7 minutes at 100 °C**. They last about **200 days** on soil or pasture, **10–180 days** in food, and **4 years in egg powder**, which is why egg powder is contraindicated in food. Common disinfectants kill them in **30 minutes to 2 hours**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** The antigenic formula has **three parts: the O antigen, phase 1 and phase 2 of the H antigen**.

- **O antigen**: over **60 different O structures** defining **46 O serogroups**. They're labeled with capital letters (**A…Z**) or only with the factor numbers (**O51…O67**).
- **Vi antigen** (envelope, "virulence"): found in **S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi C**. It's a polysaccharide, a **homopolymer of N-acetylgalactosaminuronic acid**. It **covers and masks the O antigen**, preventing O agglutination. It's **the receptor for the lytic bacteriophages**, which is essential for phage typing of this serovar. It also causes leukopenia, prevents C3b binding, inhibits phagocytosis and allows multiplication inside phagocytes.
- **H antigen**: flagellar, protein, type-determining, with **phase variation**. Most serovars alternately express two types of flagella with different antigenic specificities, phase 1 and phase 2. Since **1986** we've known that some strains can express **three or more**.
- **Antigenic variation by transduction**: loss of H (strains become non-motile), loss of O (colonies go S to R), and partial or total loss of Vi.
- **Fimbrial antigens**: antibodies against **type 1 fimbriae**, which appear in typhoid patients or vaccinees, **can give false positives in quantitative serum analysis**.

**MAYA:** Then the Kauffmann-White scheme, Buiuc 31-14.

**ALEX:** The groups you need:
- **Group A**: Paratyphi A, O 1,2,12; H1 a.
- **Group B**: Paratyphi B and **Typhimurium** (O 1,4,5,12; H1 i; H2 1,2), plus Derby, Agona, Heidelberg.
- **Group C1**: Paratyphi C (6,7,Vi), Choleraesuis, Infantis, Virchow.
- **Group C2**: Newport, Muenchen.
- **Group D1**: **Typhi (O 9,12,Vi; H1 d; no phase 2)**, **Enteritidis (O 1,9,12; H1 g,m)**, Dublin, Gallinarum, Pullorum.
- **Group E1**: Anatum, London.
- **Group F**: Aberdeen.
- Plus groups G, I, L, O, R, S.

**MAYA:** So Typhi: O 9, 12, Vi; H d; monophasic.

**ALEX:** Exactly. Memorize that formula.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- Pathogenic through **multiplication and invasiveness**.
- **Colonization** is a mandatory step, done by adhesins and fimbriae that induce changes in the cell membrane. This was the first proof that a hormone receptor could be affected by bacterial attachment and invasion.
- **Invasion genes**: a set of genes identified in *Salmonella*, some similar to *Yersinia* genes.
- **Intracellular survival**: through surface structures and the synthesis of **over 40 different proteins** that appear in response to phagocytosis. **Vi** helps resist phagocytosis and survive after it (Typhi, Paratyphi C).
- **Endotoxin** (O antigen, LPS): a major role in pathogenicity and in the **general symptoms, fever and shock**.
- **"Enterotoxins"**: in strains that cause food poisoning, with antigenic and biological properties **similar to cholera toxin**.
- *Modern layer*: **pathogenicity islands SPI-1** (enterocyte invasion, type III secretion) and **SPI-2** (survival in the macrophage vacuole).

### Clinical significance: three forms of human salmonellosis

**MAYA:** This is where the pathogenesis comes in.

**ALEX:**

**1. Systemic salmonelloses, the enteric fevers (typhoid and paratyphoid fever).** Well-defined clinical entities with a characteristic course, followed by **lasting immunity**. **Infective dose around 10³.**

The pathogenesis is a chain:
1. Attachment and passage through or between the **ileocecal epithelial cells**.
2. Multiplication in the **submucosa**.
3. Phagocytosis by **macrophages**, where the bacilli **stay alive and multiply** (intestinal lymphoid formations).
4. **Mesenteric lymph nodes**.
5. **Thoracic duct**.
6. **First bacteremia**, which marks **clinical onset**.
7. Invasion of other organs and lymphatic formations.
8. Multiplication in the **reticuloendothelial system** (liver, spleen).
9. **A second, massive bacteremia.**
10. **Elimination through bile and/or urine.**

At the **end of the first week** salmonella appears in feces, and excretion continues into convalescence. **Cholangitis and cholecystitis** can occur, and **the patient can become a carrier after recovery**. The general symptoms (fever, shock) come from **endotoxin**. The digestive ones (anorexia, pain, constipation or diarrhea) come from the intestinal, hepatic and gallbladder injury.

**2. Enteric salmonelloses: enterocolitis, gastroenteritis, infectious-type food poisoning.** The common form, widespread and endemic-epidemic. It's a large part of the bacterial diarrheal syndrome, **mainly in children up to 3 years**. **Infective dose 10⁵–10⁸.**

The mechanism: attachment and adsorption onto epithelial cells of the **terminal small intestine**, penetration into the lamina propria and ileocecal region, multiplication in the **lymphoid follicles** with RES hypertrophy and hyperplasia, PMN accumulation, inflammation and **prostaglandin release**, which stimulates intestinal secretion. The result is **non-bloody diarrhea**, nausea, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain, myalgia and headache. **Contagiousness lasts about 3 months.** Symptoms are more severe in **children under 10** and the immunocompromised (HIV/AIDS). With poor defenses, **secondary localizations** can occur: meningeal, pulmonary, bone.

**3. Salmonella septicemias.** Complications of the enteric forms, generalized after a strong insult in immunodeficient patients: children, and elderly people weakened by chronic or malignant disease. **Recorded mortality 10–20%.**

**MAYA:** That follows from the pathogenesis. The first week is bacteremic, and the end of the first week is when it reaches the gut through the bile.

**ALEX:** Macroscopically: **watery, non-bloody** diarrhea. Microscopically: wet mount of feces, where you may see mononuclear granulocytes and inflammatory cells. **No stained smears from stool.**

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:** The **isolation scheme**: inoculate an **enrichment broth** (**sodium acid selenite broth**, or alternatively **Müller-Kauffmann tetrathionate broth**, which grows promptly in 24 h and even faster at 40 °C) **and two selective-differential agars**, **MacConkey and ADCL/XLD**.

**ALEX:** Chromogenic media: **Rambach, SM ID**. And a note: some sensitive serovars don't grow or grow poorly on media with a lot of brilliant green.

**4a. Morphology.** Gram-negative rods, 0.6 by 2–4 µm, motile with peritrichous flagella except Gallinarum/Pullorum. Other serovars from clinical isolates can occasionally be non-motile too.

**4b. Culture characters.** **S-type, lactose-negative colonies of 1–3 mm**:
- **colorless and semi-transparent on MacConkey**;
- **colorless with a black center on ADCL**;
- **red, semi-transparent, with a black center on XLD**;
- **black with a metallic halo on Wilson-Blair**.

As cultures "age" they become R colonies. *S.* Paratyphi B can produce M colonies. On routine media and blood agar there's usually no hemolysis. **Careful: *S.* Typhi colonies may lack the black center on ADCL or XLD.**

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + with gas (**no gas in *S.* Typhi**), lactose/sucrose –, **H₂S +**.
- MILF/MIU: motile +, **indole –**, **LDC +**, PAD –, **urease –**.
- **Citrate +**, **VP –**, **ODC +**.

**MAYA:** The Typhi peculiarities.

**ALEX:** These are a must:
- **no gas from glucose**;
- **H₂S in small amounts and late** (± at 48 h);
- **Simmons citrate negative**;

- **motile**.

**MAYA:** And the other exceptions, Table 31-16.

**ALEX:**

**4d. Antigenic characters. This is the defining step.**

**ALEX:** **Serotyping by the Kauffmann-White scheme.**

**O antigens:**
- Bacterial suspensions inactivated by heat (**1 hour at 100 °C**) or by **90° ethanol** (**2 hours at room temperature**).
- Start with **polyvalent "O" mixtures** (polyvalent O I, polyvalent O II). Then identify the group with **monovalent "O" sera**: **AO, BO, DO, EO, LO / CO, FO, GO, HO**.

**H antigens:**
- Suspensions inactivated with **5‰ formalin**, kept at room temperature **at least 6 hours**.
- Use polyvalent "H" mixtures I and II (each with 5–7 monovalent sera). Identify **phase H1 first**, with type-specific sera (**a, b, d, e, i, Z₁₀, Z₂₉; k, r, y, z**), then **phase H2** (complexes **g; e,n; l; Z₄; Z₆**).
- **H agglutinations are done in tubes.**

**Anti-Vi serum:**
- If the strain **agglutinates only with Vi serum**, test it additionally with **Hd serum**. If it agglutinates with both, that's a **presumptive result: "*S. enterica* ser. Typhi"**, and the strain goes to the CNRS.

**MAYA:** The three interpretations.

**ALEX:**
- (a) **agglutinates with polyvalent O**: assign group and serovar;
- (b) **biochemically *Salmonella* but doesn't agglutinate with polyvalent O sera**: complete the tests and send to the CNRS;
- (c) **agglutinates with isotonic saline and with all sera**: that's "***Salmonella enterica* subsp. *enterica*, R form**."

**MAYA:** Practical tips.

**ALEX:** O identification may require prior inactivation (heat, acid or acetone). H identification uses formaldehyde inactivation. **The Vi antigen can interfere with O identification.** For speed, live cultures can be used on the slide, but there's a risk of wrong assignment from cross-agglutination.

**4e. Pathogenicity.** Not routine. Experimental pathogenicity in the mouse is a research tool.

**4f. Virulence.** *Modern layer*: PCR for SPI-1/SPI-2 genes, ***invA*** (the classic target for genus detection), ***viaB*** (Vi) and ***spvC*** (virulence plasmid).

**4g. Phage typing.**

**ALEX:**
- Phage typing has been extended to **over 30 serovars**.
- **In Romania, phage typing is routinely done for Typhi, Paratyphi B, Typhimurium and Enteritidis**, using international phage sets from the **International Centre for Enteric Phage Typing, London**, and for **Gallinarum** with a domestic phage set. Serovars **Anatum and Panama** had national schemes.
- **The Vi antigen is the receptor for the lytic phages** used to type serovar Typhi. There are four categories:
  - **Vi-typeable**;
  - **Vi-degraded**: lysis-sensitive but not assignable to a precise lysovar;
  - **Vi-untypeable**: they have Vi but can't be typed with the international set;
  - **Vi-negative**: untypeable.
- **Over 100 lysovars** are defined for ser. Typhi.
- Cumbersome as it is, phage typing is widely used for its discriminatory power and the stability of the spectra and phage preparations. **In many cases it has proved superior to molecular typing methods.**

**4h. Other typing methods.**

- **Biotyping**: the joint **Kauffmann and Petersen** scheme, with **14 tests**: arabinose, dulcitol, inositol, rhamnose, trehalose, xylose, glycerol cleavage (Stern method), H₂S and gelatinase production, and metabolism of d-, l- and i-tartrate, citrate and sodium mucate. It's useful for separating serovars with identical or very similar antigenic structure:
  - **Paratyphi B / Java** (O:1,4,5,12; H:b; 1,2);
  - **Paratyphi C / Choleraesuis / Typhisuis** (O:6,7; H:c; 1,5);
  - **Newport / Kottbus** (O:6,8; H:e,h; 1,2/1,5).
- **Plasmid typing**, 3 techniques: plasmid profile, plasmid fingerprinting (restriction endonucleases), and identification of virulence plasmid genes. Its value has proved fairly limited, but it's useful especially for distinctions within a serovar.

**4i. New-generation methods.**

- **Ribotyping**: applied to intraserovar typing of strains with a single plasmid. Useful for ser. Typhi and ser. Dublin, or for distinctions within a lysovar. **Not discriminatory enough on its own**. It's recommended together with other molecular methods, such as IS200 fingerprinting.
- **IS200 fingerprinting**: studies the distribution of a chromosomal DNA sequence thought to be specific for *Salmonella*. It discriminates well within serovars that have **no phage typing scheme and no plasmids**, such as Paratyphi B biovar Java and Typhimurium. **For ser. Enteritidis the results were unsatisfactory: only 3 patterns across 27 distinct lysovars.**
- **PFGE**: separates fragments from 10 kb to 6,000 kb, giving the "fingerprint" of the whole genome. Its use is expanding, in combination with ribotyping and IS200 fingerprinting.

**MAYA:** And the unconventional direct detection methods, Table 31-18.

**ALEX:**
- **Metabolic methods**: conductance measurement (**BACTOMETER, MALTHUS, RABIT, BACTRACE**), and metabolism of radiolabeled substrates (**BACTEC**).
- **Immunological methods**: direct and indirect immunofluorescence, latex agglutination (**WELCOLEX SALM**), coagglutination, **ELISA** (QTROL SALM, BIO ENZABED, SALMONELLA TEK, TECTRA SALM VIZUAL, VIDAS ELISA AUTOMATIC), and **piezoelectric immunosensors** (sensitivity **1–5 CFU/g**).
- Advantages: rapid, automated screening. Disadvantages: special media and equipment, cost, and sometimes mediocre specificity.

**MAYA:** And why look for alternatives at all? Table 31-17.

**ALEX:** The drawbacks of conventional methods:
- **long duration, 3–5 days**, which makes the diagnosis late and of no practical use, and keeps food in storage for a long time;
- **low sensitivity, 10²–10³ CFU/g**, which gives false negatives;
- high material consumption;
- the need for skilled labor.

**ALEX:** *Modern layer*:
- **MALDI-TOF identifies the genus, not the serovar.**
- **WGS with *in silico* serotyping** (SeqSero2, SISTR) and cgMLST has progressively replaced PFGE in surveillance networks (PulseNet, ECDC), with simultaneous detection of resistance genes.
- Multiplex PCR from stool (syndromic GI panels). Latex agglutination and PCR directly from the specimen for *S.* Typhi are **methods under study, mentioned in the course**.

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- **In enterocolitis**, an antibiogram is generally recommended only for **patients at extremes of age** or for **resistance surveillance**. Disk diffusion.
- **For every *S.* Typhi isolate, susceptibility testing is recommended.**
- **A key EUCAST rule: *Salmonella* and *Shigella* may look "susceptible" *in vitro* to aminoglycosides, first- and second-generation cephalosporins and cephamycins, but these are clinically ineffective in systemic infections and are not reported as susceptible.** That's because the bacteria survive intracellularly.
- Watch for **fluoroquinolone resistance**. Screen with **pefloxacin or nalidixic acid** for *gyrA* mutations: "nalidixic-resistant" strains respond poorly to ciprofloxacin. Also watch for ESBLs and azithromycin resistance.
- **The first ciprofloxacin-resistant *S.* Typhi was identified in 2005** (also mentioned in the course). The current problems are the **MDR H58 clone and the XDR strains from Pakistan**.

---

### Serological (indirect) diagnosis

**MAYA:** Now the reason *Salmonella* is special.

**ALEX:** **Typhoid and paratyphoid fever are the only major serological indication among enterobacteria.**

- Some authors consider **no serological test specific, sensitive and fast enough**. **The definitive diagnosis remains isolation and identification of *S.* Typhi.**
- **The Widal reaction** was devised at the **end of the 19th century** and standardized in the **mid-20th century**. **Widal with live cultures is no longer done today.**
- **The current method is quantitative serum analysis by tube agglutination.** Separate series of tubes are used, **one series per known antigen**, with the serum diluted according to the protocol. For suspected typhoid or paratyphoid, **6 series of tubes with known, inactivated antigens** are used:
  - **anti-O: TO (*S.* Typhi), AO (*S.* Paratyphi A), BO (*S.* Paratyphi B)**;
  - **anti-H: d (*S.* Typhi), a (*S.* Paratyphi A), b (*S.* Paratyphi B)**.

**MAYA:** Incubation conditions?

**ALEX:**
- **Anti-O tubes: 18–20 hours at 52 °C**, then 10 minutes at room temperature.
- **Anti-H tubes: 2 hours at 52 °C**, then 10 minutes at room temperature.
- Then the reaction is read.

**MAYA:** Titers.

**ALEX:** **Suggestive titers: 1/250 for anti-O and 1/2,500 for anti-H.** A **fourfold rise** between two successive determinations, **7–10 days apart**, confirms the suspicion.

**MAYA:** And the vaccinated?

**ALEX:** **In previously vaccinated people serology can NOT be used**, because the results can't be interpreted. **Isolation in culture is strictly necessary.**

**MAYA:** Anti-Vi?

**ALEX:** **Anti-Vi antibodies are recommended both in patients and, above all, in convalescents and carriers**, by tube agglutination. **A titer above 1/40 can be considered suggestive.**

**MAYA:** And the immune response?

**ALEX:** Anti-O antibodies (IgM, which appear first) and anti-Vi antibodies are protective. Secretory IgA can prevent attachment to the intestinal epithelium.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Salmonella enterocolitis is self-limiting and is NOT treated with antibiotics.** Antibiotics **prolong carriage and the excretion period, increase the frequency of carriage, and select resistant strains**. Antibiotics are used only for **complications, chronic disease, immunosuppression or extremes of age** (ampicillin, Biseptol, chloramphenicol). **Fluid and electrolyte rebalancing is essential.**
- **Typhoid fever** is treated pathogenically and with antibiotics: **ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin or azithromycin**, until the antibiogram is available.
- **Prevention**: hygiene (handwashing, proper food preparation) and **periodic stool-culture checks of food-industry staff**.
- **Three types of typhoid vaccine, with 50–80% protection:**
  1. **Vi polysaccharide vaccine (i.m.)**;
  2. **oral live attenuated Ty21a vaccine**;
  3. **inactivated whole-cell vaccine, the most reactogenic**.

---

### Recap

**MAYA:**
- Two species, *S. enterica* (6 subspecies) and *S. bongori*. Serovars in roman type.
- Lactose –, H₂S +, citrate +, LDC +, ODC +, indole –, urease –, VP –. Typhi has no gas, little late H₂S, citrate –, ODC –. Paratyphi A is LDC – and only 10% H₂S +.
- Kauffmann-White: Typhi = O 9,12,Vi; H d. Vi masks O and is the phage receptor.
- Typhoid: dose 10³, two bacteremias, blood in week 1, then stool, urine, bile, marrow. Enteritis: dose 10⁵–10⁸, no antibiotics.
- Selenite or Müller-Kauffmann, plus MacConkey and ADCL/XLD. Wilson-Blair for carriers.
- Serology: 6 tube series (TO, AO, BO; d, a, b), 52 °C, titers 1/250 O and 1/2,500 H, a fourfold rise, useless after vaccination. Anti-Vi >1/40 in carriers.
- Phage typing: over 100 Typhi lysovars. EUCAST: don't report aminoglycosides or first- and second-generation cephalosporins as active.

## Episode 6 — *Shigella*: One Hundred Bacteria and a Dysentery

*Hosts: **ALEX** and **MAYA***

---

**[INTRO MUSIC]**

**MAYA:** Welcome back. Today's organism needs only about a hundred cells to make you ill: *Shigella*, the intestinal pathogen *par excellence*.

**ALEX:** It's also one of the few bacterial diarrheas you **must** treat with antibiotics. Let's go through it.

---

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** Molecular biology says *Shigella* should share a genus with *Escherichia*, the "*Escherichia-Shigella*" genus. For historical and practical reasons it's still treated separately.

**MAYA:** And the four subgroups.

**ALEX:** They're defined by antigenic structure, **subgroups A to D**:
- **A: *S. dysenteriae***, with **10–13 serotypes**. **Type 1 = *Shigella shiga***, the most pathogenic. Type 2 = *S. schmitzii*. Types 3–7 = the **Large-Sachs** group.
- **B: *S. flexneri***, with **6 serotypes** subdivided into subserotypes (1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b, 4a, 4b, 5a, 5b, 6) and the **x, y variants**. Synonym: *S. paradysenteriae*.
- **C: *S. boydii***, with **15–18 serotypes**.
- **D: *S. sonnei***, with **one serotype** and **2 phases or variants, S and R**. Old names: Duval's bacillus, *Bacillus ceylonensis* A, *B. pseudodysenteriae* Kruse type E.

**MAYA:** And they're sometimes called serovars.

**ALEX:** Yes. Because anti-*Shigella* sera settle the identification where biochemical tests can't, some authors regard the *Shigella* species as "serovars."

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- ***Shigella* is NOT part of the normal intestinal flora.** It's found in the intestine and stool of the **sick human**. The reservoir is exclusively **humans and primates**.
- Depending on conditions it can be isolated from water, food or objects, and it can be **carried by flies**.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- Gram-negative rods, **non-motile**, **2–3 by 0.5–0.8 µm**, non-capsulated, non-sporulating, oxidase-negative, **lactose-negative**.
- Facultative anaerobes. **Glucose-positive WITHOUT gas**, **no H₂S**, **urease-negative**, **lysine decarboxylase-negative**. Some groups produce indole and β-galactosidase. They use sodium acetate only occasionally.
- In practice they're **very hard to distinguish from inactive (non-motile) *E. coli***.

**MAYA:** And how long they survive.

**ALEX:** They're inactivated in **10 minutes at 50–60 °C**. They survive about **10 days in dry dust**, **more than 2 weeks on linen**, **about 9 days in water at 7–10 °C**, **1–2 weeks in food**, and **up to 2 months in ice**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- The subgroups are defined by the structure of the **O antigen** (LPS). Some strains also have **K-type surface structures**.
- Within the 4 species/subgroups, types are defined by **numbered antigenic factors**. In ***S. flexneri*** some types are split into subtypes by a "group" antigen shared by several types. Typing uses **polyvalent species sera and type sera**, and for *S. flexneri* **absorbed group sera**.
- ***S. dysenteriae* type 1 also makes a heat-labile protein exotoxin, the Shiga toxin.**

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- Pathogenic through **multiplication and invasiveness**, and in *S. shiga* also through **toxin production**.

**MAYA:** The four stages of invasion.

**ALEX:**
1. **Non-receptor-specific attachment** to the host cell, with changes in the cytoskeleton. An adhesin has been reported only inconsistently, but the ***ipaD* plasmid is always present**.
2. **Plasmid-encoded IpaB and IpaC proteins induce endocytosis** of the bacterium. **Lysis of the phagosome** then allows invasion and **active multiplication in the cytoplasm**.
3. **IcsA and IcsB proteins promote intercellular spread**. "Ics" stands for *intercellular spread*.
4. **Cell death by inhibition of protein synthesis**, by a mechanism not yet understood.

The pathogenesis of the **colonic mucosal ulcerations is the same as for EIEC**.

- **Endotoxin** (O antigen): an **irritant action** in the intestine.
- **Shiga toxin** (produced by *S. dysenteriae* and released by bacteriolysis):
  - it has **no role in invasion**, but it does have a role in **vascular pathology**;
  - it **blocks protein synthesis**;
  - by its cytotoxic effect on **capillary endothelium**, it causes the **vascular complications and hemolytic uremic syndrome** of shigellosis;
  - it also affects the **CNS**: meningism, loss of consciousness, coma;
  - it's lethal in experimental animals.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- **Bacillary dysentery**, a specifically human disease characterized by a **muco-bloody diarrheal syndrome**. It rarely becomes invasive, but in marked biological decline it can cause septicemia.
- **Very low infective dose: only 100 bacteria.** **Incubation 2–5 days.**
- **Sudden onset**: fever, watery diarrhea and intense abdominal pain. Then **very frequent stools (20–40 a day), non-fecal, with mucus, pus and blood**, intestinal colic and **rectal tenesmus**. There are **abscesses** in the wall of the large intestine and terminal ileum, progressing to ulceration, necrosis and hemorrhage.
- **The main problem is fluid and electrolyte loss and acute dehydration.** Without rehydration it can be fatal.

**MAYA:** Severity by species.

**ALEX:**
- ***S. sonnei***: milder, shorter forms;
- ***S. flexneri***: more severe forms;
- ***S. dysenteriae***: **large epidemics**, with disease of particular severity.

**MAYA:** Complications?

**ALEX:** Extraintestinal: **neurological disturbances, hemolytic uremic syndrome and reactive arthritis**. *S. flexneri* has also been reported as a cause of **keratitis**. *Shigella* can also cause infectious-type food poisoning.

---

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

#### 1. Collection and transport

**ALEX:**
- It's a **diagnostic emergency**, because of the implications for the patient and for public health.
- Specimen: **feces** (possibly vomit). Collect **as soon as possible after onset**, when **10³–10⁹ bacteria/g** are shed initially, and before antibiotics.
- Macroscopically: **small amounts of stool with mucus, pus and blood**. Sometimes the blood isn't visible. **Collect and culture especially the fragments containing mucus, pus and blood.**
- Ideally, **inoculate at the bedside**. Otherwise use **Cary-Blair**.

**MAYA:** And the alternative collection methods.

**ALEX:**
- **The Nelaton catheter** is inserted into the sigmoid colon, **10–15 cm in children and 15–20 cm in adults**, and the specimen is aspirated with a syringe. It's useful in atypical cases or for checking carrier status.
- **Rectosigmoidoscopy.**
- Feces are suspended in saline or Cary-Blair.

#### 2. Macro and micro

**ALEX:**
- Macroscopically: **muco-purulent, bloody stool in small amounts**.
- Microscopically: a **fresh wet mount between slide and coverslip** (coprocytology, or the coprocytogram). **NO smears are made.** Examine with the **40× objective**. **Numerous PMNs** support an invasive mechanism. **Over 75% PMNs, together with red cells, suggests bacterial dysentery.** It's a fast, cheap and very useful test.
- Some authors recommend **direct immunofluorescence**, which is expensive because there are so many serotypes.

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:**
- *Shigella* grows on **weakly and moderately selective media**. It does **NOT multiply on highly selective media**, and it's **partly inhibited in enrichment media**.

**MAYA:** So no enrichment.

**ALEX:** Right. **Isolation is direct**, by plating on weakly and moderately selective media (**MacConkey, ADCL, XLD, SS**) **without prior enrichment**. That's the opposite of *Salmonella*.
- When lactose-negative colonies appear, **pick 3–5 isolated colonies** and subculture them onto multitest media (TSI, MIU, MILF) or API strips.

#### 4. Identification

**4a. Morphology.** Gram-negative rods, 0.5–0.8 by 2–3 µm, **non-motile**, non-capsulated, non-sporulating.

**4b. Culture.** **S-type, lactose-negative, semi-transparent colonies of 1–2 mm** on MacConkey or ADCL. *S. sonnei* can produce colonies that **turn pink** because it ferments lactose late. **Red on XLD.** A typical "lactose-negative" group.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + **WITHOUT gas**, lactose/sucrose –, **H₂S –**.
- MIU/MILF: **non-motile**, indole **variable**, **urease –**, **LDC –**, PAD –.
- **Simmons citrate –**.

**MAYA:** Versus *Escherichia* (Table 31-8)?

**ALEX:** *Shigella* produces **no gas from glucose**, **no lysine decarboxylase**, and it's **non-motile**. **Inactive *E. coli* is very hard to separate and needs anti-*Shigella* sera.**

**MAYA:** Separating the subgroups.

**ALEX:** **Mannitol fermentation.** **Subgroup A (*S. dysenteriae*) is mannitol-negative**, and the rest are mannitol-positive.

**MAYA:** And the 6-test table, 31-9.

**ALEX:** Indole, ODC, mannitol, xylose, rhamnose, ONPG:
- ***S. dysenteriae***: **all negative**. Exceptions: indole in serovars 2, 7, 8; xylose in 8, 10; rhamnose in 2, 7; **ONPG + in *S. dysenteriae* 1**.
- ***S. flexneri***: indole d, **mannitol +**, xylose – (except serovars 2, 4, 9, 12, 13, 15), rhamnose –, ODC – (except serovar 6).
- ***S. boydii***: indole d, mannitol +, ODC – (except serovar 13), rhamnose – (except serovars 2, 4, 9, 12, 13, 18).
- ***S. sonnei***: **ODC +, mannitol +, rhamnose +, ONPG +**. Those are the traits that set it clearly apart.

**ALEX:** **The 6 tests can't draw a sharp line between species.** **Serological typing** resolved these borderline cases, and additional biochemical tests may be needed.

**4d. Antigens.**
- **Polyvalent subgroup sera and type-specific sera**, by **slide agglutination**, according to the established schemes (the *Shigella* antigenic scheme, Buiuc 31-13). For *S. flexneri*, **absorbed group sera**.
- If biochemistry suggests *Shigella* but agglutination is negative, **keep a dense suspension 30–60 minutes at 100 °C**, then repeat. Heat removes the K-type envelope that masks O.
- **Latex agglutination** is a rapid screening method with **remarkable results in *Shigella***, which is present in large numbers in stool during the acute phase. It detects **10^4.5 organisms/ml**. The sera can be adsorbed onto ***S. aureus* with protein A** (coagglutination), onto **charcoal particles (India ink)**, or onto latex. It works **directly on stool and on colonies from isolation media**.

**4e. Pathogenicity: the Sereny test.**

**ALEX:** It produces **keratoconjunctivitis in the guinea pig**. Subculture the suspect colony on a slant. **After 8 hours, put a loopful of culture under the guinea pig's eyelid.** After **12–24 hours**, a positive reaction shows **conjunctival congestion, purulent discharge and corneal opacity**, progressing to keratoconjunctivitis.

**MAYA:** And the warning.

**ALEX:** **The test can also be positive with some *E. coli* strains (EIEC).** Invasiveness *in vitro* is shown by **intracellular growth in HeLa cells**.

**4f. Virulence.** *Modern layer*: PCR for ***ipaH***, which is present in **both *Shigella* and EIEC and doesn't separate them**; *ipaB/C*; *icsA*; and PCR for ***stx1*** in *S. dysenteriae* type 1.

**4g. Phage typing.** Used in epidemiological or research studies and reserved for **reference labs**. Schemes were developed especially for the epidemiologically dominant serotypes: ***S. flexneri* 2a and *S. sonnei***. **Phage typing has often been shown superior to biotyping or resistotyping.**

**4h. Other typing.**
- **Biotyping** is little used, given the genus's **modest metabolic activity**. **ONPG and ornithine decarboxylase** work as a **screening system to separate *S. sonnei*** from the other serovars (dysenteriae, flexneri, boydii), which are **consistently inactive on these two tests**. It applies only in outbreaks where *S. sonnei* is already known to be the cause.
- **Bacteriocin typing (colicin typing)** was promoted for *S. sonnei*. The **lability of the preparations and the instability of the bacteriocin types** reduced interest in it.
- **Resistotyping** is one of the most accessible methods, recommended and often used in epidemiological investigations. It's limited by its dependence on many technical variables and by variation in the spectrum even within one outbreak. **Plasmid profiling with identification of resistance plasmids replaces it, with clearly better results.**

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- Molecular biology: **plasmid profiling, ribotyping, DNA probes**.
- *Modern layer*: **MALDI-TOF does not separate *Shigella* from *E. coli***, so identification is **confirmed serologically**. Multiplex PCR (GI panels) detects the shared ***ipaH*** target, reported as "*Shigella*/EIEC." **WGS/cgMLST** is used for outbreaks, including **MDR clones transmitted sexually among men who have sex with men**.

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- **Standardized disk diffusion**, both for **resistance surveillance** and for **choosing treatment**. The antibiogram has become necessary because resistant strains have emerged.
- As with *Salmonella*: **aminoglycosides and first- and second-generation cephalosporins are not reported as susceptible.**
- **Frequent resistance to ampicillin and cotrimoxazol.** For example, the course mentions an ***S. sonnei* strain resistant to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole identified in 2005 in the USA**. Resistance to **ciprofloxacin and azithromycin** is increasingly frequent, and so are **ESBLs (CTX-M-15)**.

---

### Serological diagnosis

**ALEX:**
- **Serological diagnosis is possible**, by detecting a **rising antibody titer** with tube agglutination, but it has **no practical importance**.
- The infection stimulates a humoral response, but **the antibodies are not protective**. **Local IgA antibodies**, possibly after live attenuated vaccines, might be useful for prevention.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Bacterial dysentery is one of the few bacterial acute diarrheal diseases that MUST be treated with antibiotics**, together with fluid and electrolyte rebalancing. Options: **ciprofloxacin, ampicillin/amoxicillin, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole**.
- **Do NOT give drugs that inhibit intestinal peristalsis.** They **prolong the diarrhea, the fever and the excretion period**.

**MAYA:** Epidemiology.

**ALEX:**
- **Source**: the sick human, symptomatic or not.
- **Fecal-oral transmission**. Remember the **"4 F's"**. In the Romanian course they're the "4 M's": *mâini murdare, muşte, mâncare, materii fecale*, which means **dirty hands, flies, food, feces**. Transmission by **sexual contact** is also possible.
- **The ratio of foodborne to waterborne outbreaks is about 2 to 1.**
- **Children under 10** are mainly affected, **especially ages 0–5**.
- Poor sanitation drives spread.

### Recap

**MAYA:**
- Four subgroups: A *dysenteriae* (mannitol –, type 1 = Shiga toxin), B *flexneri* (2a dominant), C *boydii*, D *sonnei* (ODC +, ONPG +, rhamnose +, late lactose).

- Stool: culture the mucus and blood flecks, bedside inoculation or Cary-Blair, Nelaton catheter. Wet mount with over 75% PMNs. **Direct plating, no enrichment.**
- Slide agglutination; if negative, boil 30–60 minutes. Latex 10^4.5/ml. Sereny test, also positive for EIEC. MALDI-TOF can't tell it from *E. coli*.

## Episode 7 — *Yersinia*: The Plague, the Pig and the Pseudo-Appendix

*Hosts: **ALEX** and **MAYA***

**[INTRO MUSIC]**

**MAYA:** Welcome back. Today's genus runs from the Black Death to a child having an unnecessary appendectomy: *Yersinia*.

**ALEX:** It's also the genus where temperature matters most. Motility depends on it, enrichment depends on it, and even capsule expression depends on it.

---

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** The genus has **11–12 species**, and **3 are pathogenic for humans**:
- ***Y. pestis***, the agent of plague;
- ***Y. pseudotuberculosis***;
- ***Y. enterocolitica***.

The others (*Y. frederiksenii, Y. kristensenii, Y. intermedia, Y. mollaretii, Y. bercovieri, Y. rohdei, Y. aldovae, Y. ruckeri*) are found in soil and water or are pathogenic for wild mammals, birds and fish.

**MAYA:** The name.

**ALEX:** It honors **Alexandre Yersin**, who **isolated the plague bacillus in Hong Kong in 1894**. *Modern layer*: the genus is in the family ***Yersiniaceae***, and ***Y. pestis* is a recently derived clone of *Y. pseudotuberculosis***.

### Habitat

**ALEX:** All species are widespread in the environment, especially in **soil**, where they survive a long time (over 3 months).

- ***Y. pestis***: the main reservoir is **wild rodents**: rats, field mice, squirrels, rabbits, moles, and in the US, prairie dogs. It reaches humans through **the rat flea, *Xenopsylla cheopis***, in whose **proventriculus and stomach it survives for a long time**. It also spreads through dust from droppings or through animal products. **Between humans** it's transmitted by ***Pulex irritans*** and, frequently, by **inhaling Flügge droplets** from a patient in the prodromal or acute phase of **pneumonic plague**.
- ***Y. pseudotuberculosis***: the natural reservoir is **wild rodents and birds**. It reaches soil and water and survives actively there, even at low temperatures.
- ***Y. enterocolitica***: the natural reservoir is **wild and domestic mammals** (sheep, **pigs**, cows, dogs, cats). **The pig is one of the important sources for humans.** It has been isolated from a wide range of foods contaminated during processing, where it stays biologically active for a long time: chilled or processed meat, milk, cheeses, **even chocolate**. It's a **psychrotrophic** bacterium, meaning it grows in the cold.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Coccobacillary** enterobacteria, **0.5–0.8 by 1–3 µm**, pleomorphic, non-sporulating, with **bipolar staining**. **Giemsa staining is recommended, especially for *Y. pestis***.
- In living organisms and young cultures *Y. pestis* has a **pseudocapsular envelope**. At **37 °C, *in vivo* and on rich media, it develops a capsule**.
- **Temperature-dependent motility**: **non-motile at 37 °C, motile at 22–30 °C**, by peritrichous flagella, when grown below 30 °C. **Exception: *Y. pestis* is always non-motile, at any temperature.**

**MAYA:** Metabolism.

**ALEX:**
- They **ferment glucose without gas**.
- They produce **β-galactosidase (ONPG +)** but **don't acidify lactose**.
- **No H₂S, no LDC, no PAD.**
- **No growth on Simmons citrate.** **No malonate.**
- **Except *Y. pestis*, they have intense urease activity.**
- **Except *Y. pestis* and *Y. pseudotuberculosis*, they produce indole and ornithine decarboxylase.**
- They grow from **4 °C to 43 °C** and tolerate **5% salt**.
- They're not very resistant to physical and chemical agents. Outside the host they survive **up to 2 weeks in pus or sputum** and **several months in corpses**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- **O antigen** (LPS, endotoxin), as in all enterobacteria.
- ***Y. pestis***:
  - **Fraction I (F1)**: an envelope protein produced mainly **at 37 °C**, with **antiphagocytic** properties, which also activates complement;
  - **the V and W protein antigens**, chromosomally or plasmid-encoded, made by the wild virulent type;
  - **a protein exotoxin** lethal to laboratory animals;
  - **pesticin**, a bacteriocin.
  - Some *Y. pestis* antigens cross-react with those of other yersiniae.
- ***Y. enterocolitica***: **over 50 serotypes**. Most human isolates belong to **O3, O9 and O8**. **In Romania, O3 predominates.** There are geographic differences in distribution.
- ***Y. pseudotuberculosis***: **at least 6 serotypes**, and **type 1 (I)** is the one most often involved in humans.
- **Cross-reactions with antigens of *Brucella* and *Salmonella*** are a source of false-positive serology.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**

***Y. pestis*:**
- Pathogenic through **multiplication and invasiveness**. The **infective dose is tiny: 1–10 bacilli.**
- The key role belongs to structures that give **resistance to phagocytosis**: **surface protein Fraction I** and the **pseudocapsule**. Virulence falls without the pseudocapsule.
- Virulence structures are **plasmid- or chromosome-encoded**. They haven't been found in avirulent strains, which are useful for vaccines.
- **Lethal exotoxin**: a protein of **74,000 Da**, lethal at **only 1 µg in the mouse**. It produces **β-adrenergic blockade** and is **cardiotoxic** in animals. Its role in human infection hasn't been worked out.
- **Coagulase** made in the flea's gut **blocks gastric peristalsis**. We'll see why that matters in a moment.

***Y. enterocolitica*:**
- A **heat-stable enterotoxin**, whose role in human diarrhea isn't well defined.
- **Invasiveness**, localizing to the **terminal ileum** and involving the **mesenteric lymph nodes**.

*Modern layer*:
- **The pYV/pCD1 virulence plasmid (40–50 kb)**, shared by all three pathogenic species, encodes the **Ysc type III secretion system** and the **Yop effector proteins**, which block phagocytosis and cell signaling.
- **Invasin** (*inv* gene) and **Ail** (attachment/invasion, complement resistance).
- **Yersiniabactin**, the siderophore of the high-pathogenicity island (HPI).

### Clinical significance

#### A. Plague, caused by *Y. pestis*

**MAYA:** Walk me through the flea cycle. It's a beautiful piece of biology.

**ALEX:** It is.
1. The **flea feeds on an infected rodent**.
2. The bacteria reach the flea's gut and, through **coagulase, block gastric peristalsis**.
3. The **bacterial mass and fibrinous structure block transit**.
4. The **starving flea bites again and regurgitates the microorganisms**.
5. Because it can no longer feed, the flea **loses its natural preference for rodents** and ends up **biting humans**.

**MAYA:** Bubonic plague.

**ALEX:** The inoculated organism is **phagocytosed but multiplies both inside and outside cells**. It reaches the **lymph nodes** quickly, causing **hemorrhagic inflammation, enlargement, necrosis and fluctuance: the bubo**. During invasion it can enter the bloodstream and generalize, causing hemorrhagic and necrotic lesions in every organ: meningitis, pneumonia, serosanguineous pleuropericarditis. **Untreated mortality is over 50%.**

**MAYA:** Primary pneumonic plague.

**ALEX:** It comes from **inhaling droplet nuclei** from a coughing patient, or from **septic pulmonary emboli**. It causes **hemorrhagic pneumonia** and septicemia, with **death in over 90% of cases**. **Mortality is about 100% if treatment doesn't start within the first 24 hours.**

**ALEX:** *Y. pestis* is also classified as a **potential bioterrorism agent, CDC category A**.

#### B. Enteric yersinioses: *Y. enterocolitica* and *Y. pseudotuberculosis*

**ALEX:**
- **Infective dose 10⁸–10⁹. Incubation 5–10 days.**
- Multiplication in the intestinal mucosa, particularly the **ileum**. Ulcerations, local inflammation, **leukocytes in the stool**, spread to the **mesenteric lymph nodes** and, less often, to the blood.
- **In children**: enteritis and a **right iliac fossa pain syndrome**, which can lead to a **wrong diagnosis of acute appendicitis ("pseudo-appendicitis")** and **unnecessary appendectomies**.
- **In adults**: **1–2 weeks after onset**, **arthralgia, reactive arthritis and erythema nodosum**. These are a **post-infectious immunological reaction**.
- ***Y. pseudotuberculosis***: **mesenteric adenitis**, subacute enteritis, lower incidence.
- **In the immunocompromised**: complicated forms (mesenteric adenitis, appendicitis), extraintestinal forms and **septicemia**, where **gentamicin therapy is strictly necessary**.
- *Y. frederiksenii* is an intestinal commensal that can occasionally cause benign enterocolitis. *Y. ruckeri* is a **fish pathogen**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

#### 1. Collection and transport

**ALEX:**
- ***Y. pestis***: **collect with maximum precaution**, according to the clinical form: **blood, lymph node pus (bubo aspirate), sputum**. The disease carries **individual and collective risk**. **Isolation is done only in specialized labs** under a restrictive regime (**biosafety levels 3 and 4**), **always in biological safety cabinets with protective equipment**.
- **Enteric yersinioses**: **feces**, **lymph nodes taken during surgery**, blood. **Cary-Blair** transport medium.

#### 2. Macro and micro

**ALEX:**
- Macroscopically: diarrheal stool, possibly with mucus.
- Microscopically, stool gets a **fresh wet mount, no smears**, 40× objective, showing **inflammatory cells**.
- **For *Y. pestis***: smears stained **Gram, Giemsa**, and possibly **immunofluorescence**. You see **Gram-negative coccobacilli with bipolar staining, the "safety pin" appearance**.

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:**
- All species grow **from 4 to 43 °C** and are **tolerant to 5% salt**.

***Y. pestis*:**
- Aerobic, facultatively anaerobic. It grows faster on **blood or body-fluid media and at 30 °C**, but it also grows on simple media.
- On **blood agar at 37 °C** colonies are **very small** and appear after about 24 hours.
- On **plain agar**: colonies of **0.1–0.2 mm at 24 h and 1–2 mm at 48 h**, round, with slightly fringed edges. They're grey at first, with a **yellowish, domed center: the "Chinese hat"** colony.
- **In liquid media**: a **pellicle and abundant flocculent deposit**, while the rest of the medium **stays clear**.
- Virulent organisms inoculated directly from infected tissue give **S colonies, mucoid, viscous and grey-white**. **After lab passages colonies become R and the capsule disappears.**

***Y. pseudotuberculosis***: **uneven growth**, with a deposit and medium that becomes progressively cloudy from the bottom of the tube. The other species cloud the medium uniformly.

**From feces:**
- With or without enrichment, on **MacConkey agar (read after 48 hours)** or, better, on **CIN medium (Cefsulodin-Irgasan-Novobiocin)**. Incubate in a single step, **18–24 h at 37 °C or at room temperature (20–30 °C)**.

**MAYA:** And cold enrichment.

**ALEX:** **"Cold enrichment"**: inoculate the specimen into **phosphate-buffered saline at 4 °C for 1–3 weeks**, then subculture onto the media above. **Irgasan-Ticarcillin broth**, recommended for food testing, also works well for human specimens.

- The other *Yersinia* species form **lactose-negative colonies of 1–1.5 mm in 24 h at 37 °C**, reaching **2–3 mm at 48 h**.
- **Careful: some *Y. pseudotuberculosis* strains don't grow on CIN.**

#### 4. Identification

**4a. Morphology.** Pleomorphic Gram-negative coccobacilli or rods, 0.5–3 by 1–2 µm, **bipolar staining (Giemsa)**. Peritrichous flagella when grown below 30 °C. **Non-motile at 37 °C, motile at 22–30 °C.** ***Y. pestis* is a non-motile species with a pseudocapsular envelope.**

**4b. Culture.** S colonies of 1–1.5 mm (2–3 mm at 48 h), lactose-negative. **On CIN: semi-transparent colonies with clear edges and a red center, the "bull's eye."** *Y. pestis*: "Chinese hat" colonies, pellicle and flocculent deposit in broth.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- TSI: glucose + **without gas**, lactose –, **H₂S –**. **Sucrose is fermented only by *Y. enterocolitica***, which acidifies the TSI slant.
- MIU/MILF: **non-motile at 37 °C**. MILF only becomes cloudy after **24 h at room temperature**. Indole – (except some *Y. enterocolitica* strains), **urease +**, **LDC –**, **PAD –**.
- **Simmons citrate –, malonate –, ONPG +.**
- **ODC + only in *Y. enterocolitica*.**

**MAYA:** Species table, Buiuc 31-19.

**ALEX:** Percent positive, with motility tested at 37 °C:
- ***Y. pestis***: motility 0, ODC 0, **urease 5**, indole 0, sucrose 0, rhamnose 1, cellobiose 0, sorbitol 50, salicin 70.
- ***Y. pseudotuberculosis***: motility 0, ODC 0, **urease 95**, indole 0, sucrose 0, **rhamnose 70**, cellobiose 0, **sorbitol 0**, salicin 25.
- ***Y. enterocolitica***: motility 2, **ODC 95**, urease 75, **indole 50**, **sucrose 95**, rhamnose 1, **cellobiose 75**, **sorbitol 99**, salicin 20.
- ***Y. frederiksenii***: indole 100, rhamnose 99.
- ***Y. intermedia***: rhamnose 100, cellobiose 96.

**Except *Y. pestis*, all species are motile at 25 °C.**

**4d. Antigens.**
- **Slide agglutination with anti-*Y. enterocolitica* O3 and anti-*Y. pseudotuberculosis* type I sera**, the dominant serovars in Romania. This confirms genus and species.
- Serotyping for other types is needed when biochemistry places the strain in the two common species but the corresponding sera don't confirm it. **Serovars such as *Y. enterocolitica* O9 are relatively frequent in Europe and Canada.**
- *Modern layer*: for *Y. pestis*, **rapid immunochromatographic dipstick tests for F1 antigen**, usable in the field in endemic areas (WHO).

**4e.** Not routine; experimental pathogenicity in animals is done in reference labs.

**4f. Virulence.** **Genetic identification of colonies isolated on selective media (CIN)** is widely used to determine the species and, above all, **the presence of the virulence plasmid in *Y. enterocolitica* strains**.

**4g. Phage typing.** It was promoted many years ago **in Sweden and France**. Schemes were described for ***Y. enterocolitica***, but they have been **replaced by, or combined with, genetic typing**.

**4h. Other typing.**

**MAYA:** The pesticins.

**ALEX:** **Bacteriocin typing is of special interest here.** *Y. pestis* produces **two bacteriocins: pesticin I and pesticin II.**
- **Pesticin I** is active on ***Y. pseudotuberculosis*** and on some ***E. coli*** strains. Sensitivity of ***Y. enterocolitica*** to pesticin is linked to a **65 kDa membrane protein** that acts as the **receptor**.
- **Pesticin II** is active **only on *Y. pseudotuberculosis***.

**Genetic typing:**
- **REAP** (restriction endonuclease analysis of plasmids) doesn't work on strains with no plasmid or with a single plasmid.
- **REAC** (restriction of chromosomal DNA) has advantages over REAP, but the large number of bands makes comparison difficult.
- **REAC + REAP + biotyping + serotyping + resistotyping together give the most useful epidemiological information.**
- Resistotyping, biotyping and serotyping are used in combination.

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- **PFGE and MLEE** (multilocus enzyme electrophoresis), in various combinations, type *Yersinia* species **except *Y. pestis***.
- **Ribotyping with PCR analysis of the resulting fragments** is the **most effective method for typing *Y. enterocolitica***.
- Direct detection in the specimen has become established for diagnosing ***Y. pestis* bacteremia**. For the other yersiniae, direct detection in stool has the same difficulties as for salmonellae.
- **Automated systems** can be used, for example the **ATB G-5 kit**, read and interpreted after 18–24 h, with **21 antibiotics** for Gram-negative bacilli.
- *Modern layer*: MALDI-TOF (**beware the biohazard of handling *Y. pestis***), WGS/cgMLST, and PCR for ***ail, inv, yadA, virF***, and for ***caf1/pla*** in *Y. pestis*.

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- Recommended, by standardized disk diffusion. Automated systems can also be used.
- ***Y. enterocolitica***: **natural resistance to aminopenicillins, ticarcillin and first-generation cephalosporins** (chromosomal β-lactamases A and B). **Susceptible to cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, aminoglycosides and tetracyclines.**
- ***Y. pestis***: no significant natural resistance. **No resistance to streptomycin, tetracycline or chloramphenicol has yet been identified** (also mentioned in the course), although *modern layer* **strains with multiresistance plasmids have been reported in Madagascar**.

### Serological diagnosis

**MAYA:** *Yersinia* is one of the serology exceptions.

**ALEX:** Yes, for both diseases.

**A. Plague.**
- Laboratory diagnosis can be **bacteriological and serological**.
- **In patients not previously vaccinated, a titer above 1/26 is suspect.**
- Techniques: **microagglutination, complement fixation (CFT) or ELISA**.
- **A rising titer between two consecutive determinations confirms the diagnosis retrospectively.**
- There's post-infection immunity, and **vaccines give incomplete protection**.

**B. Enteric yersinioses.**
- **Serology is indicated for the extraintestinal manifestations.** It helps determine the cause of **reactive arthritis, erythema nodosum** and other extraintestinal manifestations.
- **Antibodies appear about 4–7 days after onset, peak at about 14 days, and persist for several months.**
- Technique: **tube agglutination**. **A significant titer is ≥1/160.** **Paired sera tested over time** are recommended.
- **Watch for cross-reactions with *Brucella* and *Salmonella* antigens.**

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**

**Plague:**
- **The drug of choice is streptomycin, for at least 10 days.**
- Alternatives: **tetracycline** (sometimes with streptomycin) and **chloramphenicol**. **Chloramphenicol is preferred for meningitis** and can be combined with streptomycin.
- **Prophylaxis: doxycycline or cotrimoxazole.** The US military also recommends **ciprofloxacin**.
- **Isolate patients promptly** and **notify the authorities immediately**.
- **Contacts of pneumonic plague** get **tetracycline 0.5 g/day, 5 days a week**, or doxycycline or cotrimoxazole for 7 days.
- **Plague vaccination for travelers to endemic areas.** **Surgical masks** protect against droplet nuclei.

**Enteric yersinioses:**
- **Most infections are self-limiting.** Severe forms in the immunocompromised need therapy (e.g. **gentamicin**).
- **There are no specific preventive measures.** The main source is contact with domestic or farm animals and their contaminated products.

## PART A — *VIBRIO*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** *Vibrio* used to belong to the family ***Vibrionaceae*** (1965), together with *Aeromonas*, *Photobacterium* and *Plesiomonas*. Recent molecular and chemotaxonomic studies **dispute that this family is real**. Still, their habitat and their phenotypic and pathogenic similarities justify **treating the three genera together for identification** in clinical microbiology.

**MAYA:** The hybridization data.

**ALEX:** rRNA–DNA hybridization shows **at least 80% relatedness between *Vibrio* and *Photobacterium***, but **under 60% with *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas***. That justifies separating the taxa.

**MAYA:** How many species?

**ALEX:** **Over 48 species** (36 according to Buiuc), and **at least 12** are pathogenic or potentially pathogenic for humans:
- ***V. cholerae* O:1 and O:139**, the **cholera vibrios**;
- *V. cholerae* non-O:1;
- *V. mimicus*;
- ***V. parahaemolyticus***;
- ***V. vulnificus***;
- *V. alginolyticus, V. fluvialis, V. furnissii, V. hollisae, V. damsela, V. cincinnatiensis, V. metschnikovii, V. carchariae*;
- *Photobacterium damselae*.

**ALEX:** The genus is phylogenetically heterogeneous: there's the *V. cholerae–V. mimicus* group, the *V. fluvialis–V. furnissii* complex, and the *V. parahaemolyticus–V. alginolyticus* line. *V. anguillarum* and *V. damsela* have been proposed for reclassification into *Listonella* and *Photobacterium*, respectively.

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- **Aquatic organisms**: marine, estuarine, coastal, lagoon and fresh waters. They're abundant in marine and estuarine waters, living in symbiosis with the vertebrates and invertebrates there.
- **Halophilic species** (*V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus*, etc.) are **limited to sea water**. **Non-halophilic species** (*V. cholerae, V. mimicus*) also spread into **fresh water** (rivers, lakes) far from the sea, where they're present only transiently.
- ***V. cholerae*** is found in the gut and feces of cholera patients. Its presence in the environment is explained by fecal contamination and survival, and there may also be an **extraintestinal life cycle**.

**MAYA:** And the three forms of persistence in sea water.

**ALEX:**
1. **The free form**, as long as the water provides nutrients.
2. **The epibiotic form**: close association with the **chitin matrix of zooplankton, shellfish and copepods**, helped by **chitinases** and amino-sugar binding proteins. **Epibiotic vibrios persist longer and get past the gastric acid barrier more effectively.**
3. **The "dormant" form**: **microvibrios and viable but non-culturable (VBNC)** cells, demonstrated with immunofluorescent stains.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Straight or comma-curved** Gram-negative rods. The comma shape has become "more of a historical image." They're **1.4–2.6 µm long and 0.5–0.8 µm wide**, non-sporulating, non-capsulated and **very motile**. In liquid media they move with **polar flagella**, single or multiple. **On solid media they use lateral flagella** with a shorter wavelength than the polar flagellum.
- **Catalase- and oxidase-positive.** The **exception is *V. metschnikovii*, which is oxidase-negative** because it lacks cytochrome c.
- Undemanding, facultatively anaerobic. They attack sugars **fermentatively, without gas**, and have both respiratory and fermentative metabolism.
- **Sensitive to the vibriostatic agent O/129** (2,4-diamino-6,7-diisopropylpteridine) at **10 and/or 150 µg**. This is a classic genus character, but **its value has become relative: over 90% of *V. cholerae* O:1 strains isolated in India are resistant to both concentrations.**
- They produce extracellular enzymes: cytotoxic and cytolytic toxins, neuraminidase, chitinase, protease, lipase.
- They're adapted to varied salinities. **NaCl stimulates growth in some species and is mandatory for others (halophiles).** ***V. cholerae* grows well at alkaline pH (9–9.5)**, which is the basis of the enrichment media.
- **Survival**: very sensitive to sunlight and heat (killed almost instantly at 100 °C) and to antiseptics. They live **up to 7 days in food**, a few days in water depending on sewage treatment, and **several weeks in the intestine of untreated patients**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- **O antigen** (LPS) in the cell wall. By the structure of the somatic O group antigen, cholera vibrios were split into **6 serogroups**. **The classical cholera vibrio and the El Tor biotype both belong to group O:1.** The others are **non-O:1, "NAG" (non-agglutinable** with anti-O:1 sera), which can't be told apart biochemically from O:1. Taxonomic work has shown that ***V. cholerae* O:1 and non-O:1 are in fact the same species**.
- **Serotypes within O:1**, defined by antigenic factors **a, b, c**:
  - **Ogawa (a, b)**;
  - **Inaba (a, c)**;
  - **Hikojima (a, b, c)**.
  The first two are commonly isolated and spread epidemically. Hikojima is very rare and not recognized by some specialists.
- **Serogroup O:139 ("Bengal")** is an **O mutant of the El Tor biovar**.
- **H antigen** (flagellar), since vibrios are motile. Antigens on the pili.
- **Cholera enterotoxin**: heat-labile, MW **84,000 Da**, made of **one A subunit (active) and 5 B subunits (binding)**.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **The family's pathogenic mechanism**: adherence to the epithelium (adhesins, pili), then colonization, invasion of the mucosa, and production of biologically active substances: extracellular enzymes, enterotoxins, cytotoxins and hemolysins.
- **Chemotactic capacity** toward mucosal epithelium is more evident in **monotrichous forms** and depends on the amount and quality of mucus.
- **Adhesins**: surface protein components linked to fimbriae and pili, which bind stereochemically to complementary tissue molecules. **The colonization factors are made in parallel with the enterotoxin and controlled by the same gene. The pili are lost once infection is established.**
- **The S layer** outside the cell wall, seen by electron microscopy in virulent strains, protects against lytic agents (bacteriophages, serum proteins).

**MAYA:** Now the toxin mechanism. Step by step.

**ALEX:** **Cholera enterotoxin is the main cause of secretory diarrhea.**
1. **The B subunits have high affinity for GM1 ganglioside receptors** on target cells.
2. After binding, **the A subunit enters the cytoplasm**.
3. It catalyzes **transfer of ADP-ribose from NAD**, activating the **membrane regulatory protein Gs**.
4. Activated Gs drives **increased cAMP** in the small intestinal mucosal cells.
5. This causes **hypersecretion of chloride and bicarbonate**.
6. A **massive efflux of water** follows.

**ALEX:** And the experiment: **volunteers who swallowed less than 10 µg of purified toxin developed "explosive" diarrhea of over 20 liters within 2 days.**

**MAYA:** Wow. Other toxins?

**ALEX:**
- **Thermostable direct hemolysin (TDH)** is responsible for the **Kanagawa phenomenon**. It's a **major pathogenicity factor of *V. parahaemolyticus***, together with a related cytotoxin, and it's been found in other species too.
- **The capsule** is an important virulence factor in ***V. vulnificus***.
- **Endotoxin** is released from the wall when the bacterium is destroyed.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:** **Cholera** is a gastroenteritis of major public-health importance, caused by **serogroups O:1 and O:139 of *V. cholerae***.
- **The infective dose is high: 10⁸–10¹⁰ organisms.**
- **It isn't an invasive disease.** The bacteria **don't enter the bloodstream**. They stay in the intestinal tract, colonize the **brush border** of epithelial cells, multiply and release toxin.
- **Incubation 1–4 days.** **Sudden onset** with nausea, vomiting, **profuse diarrhea (20–30 L/day)** and abdominal cramps.
- **Watery, "rice-water" stools**, with mucus, epithelial cells and **enormous numbers of vibrios**.
- Rapid fluid and electrolyte loss leads to **dehydration, circulatory collapse and anuria**. **Untreated mortality is 25–50%.**

**MAYA:** And the other species, Table 31-28.

**ALEX:**
- **Gastroenteritis**: *V. cholerae* O:1/O:139 (+++), *V. cholerae* non-O:1 (+++), *V. mimicus*, *V. fluvialis*, ***V. parahaemolyticus* (+++, after eating seafood)**, *V. hollisae*.
- **Wound infections**: *V. cholerae* non-O:1, *V. alginolyticus*, *V. vulnificus*, *V. carchariae*. These occur in **people rescued from drowning, wounds contaminated with sea water, and shark bites**.
- **Septicemia**: ***V. vulnificus* (+++)**, with risk factors of **liver disease, age over 40, and male sex**.
- **Pneumonia**: *V. vulnificus*, *V. alginolyticus*.
- **Eye and ear infections**: *V. alginolyticus*, *V. cholerae* non-O:1.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

#### 1. Collection and transport

**ALEX:**
- **It's an epidemiological emergency.** The diagnosis has to be made as fast as possible.
- Specimen: **feces** (most often) and vomit. Collect quickly, before antibiotics.
- Macroscopically: **watery, rice-water stools, greenish, foul-smelling, with mucus flakes**.
- **Transport within 1–3 hours at most.** Transport medium: **Cary-Blair**. **Alkaline peptone water (pH 9–9.5) can serve both as transport medium and as enrichment medium.**
- From wounds, in a suggestive setting (sea-water-contaminated wounds, shark bites), isolation can be done on a **non-selective medium**.

#### 2. Macro and micro

**ALEX:**
- Macroscopically: the **rice-water** look of the stool.
- Microscopically: **a fresh wet mount between slide and coverslip, NO smears**, examined at **40×**, **preferably on dark field**.

**MAYA:** And the key finding.

**ALEX:** **Essentially, NO leukocytes are seen, because cholera isn't an invasive disease.** Vibrios are present in large numbers with **active "tumbling" movements** or chaotic darting typical of monoflagellate organisms.
- **The immobilization test**: treat the specimen with **specific antibodies**. **If motility stops, the diagnosis is confirmed.**
- Grown under optimal conditions they're Gram-negative rods of 0.5–0.8 by 1.4–2.6 µm. **In young cultures curved forms predominate**, occasionally S-shaped. **As the culture ages, straight forms increase** and they become hard to distinguish from other enteric rods. **On media with suboptimal NaCl, vibrios look pleomorphic** (especially halophiles).

#### 3. Culture

**ALEX:**
- Undemanding. They grow on simple media, including **alkaline peptone water (APW)**, where they form a **surface "veil."** That's useful for primary isolation.
- **Selectivity relies on the vibrios' ability to grow at pH 8.6**, which is unfavorable to many bacteria, and to **tolerate toxic concentrations of organic compounds** (bile, bile salts) **or inorganic ones** (potassium iodide, potassium tellurite).
- Two media are used in parallel: a **moderately selective BSA (Bile Salts Agar)** and a **highly selective TCBS (Thiosulphate-Citrate-Bile salts-Sucrose)**. Selectivity varies by lot and manufacturer.

**MAYA:** The recommended scheme.

**ALEX:** **Inoculate the specimen into APW**, directly or after transport in Cary-Blair. **After 6–8 hours at 35–37 °C, plate onto the selective medium, taking 2 loopfuls from the surface of the APW**, where the veil forms.

**MAYA:** What do the colonies look like?

**ALEX:**
- **On TCBS:**
  - ***V. cholerae*: yellow colonies (sucrose fermentation), large, 2–4 mm;**
  - *V. parahaemolyticus, V. mimicus, V. hollisae*: **green** colonies;
  - *V. vulnificus*: green (85%) or yellow (15%);
  - *V. hollisae, V. damsela* and *V. cincinnatiensis* grow poorly or not at all on TCBS.
  - **Careful**: *Proteus* can grow on TCBS too, and *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas* variably.
- **On BSA**: S colonies, round, shiny and **transparent like dewdrops**, unlike *E. coli* colonies, which are matt.
- **On non-selective media with 0.5–1% NaCl**: colonies of 2–5 mm, flat or slightly convex, smooth, moist and shiny. Variants can be transparent or opaque. **Rough variants make wrinkled colonies that are hard to emulsify.**
- **Only some *Vibrio* species grow on MacConkey** (e.g. *V. cholerae*), forming **lactose-negative** colonies.
- **On 5% sheep, rabbit or horse blood agar**: *V. damsela, V. fluvialis, V. hollisae, V. metschnikovii* and, usually, **non-O:1 *V. cholerae*** strains produce **β-hemolysis** after 24 hours. **El Tor biovar strains are often β-hemolytic too.** *V. alginolyticus, V. parahaemolyticus* and *V. vulnificus* are non-hemolytic or show only a circumscribed greening.

**MAYA:** The Kanagawa phenomenon.

**ALEX:** **The Kanagawa phenomenon is β-hemolysis by *V. parahaemolyticus* on Wagatsuma agar.** Wagatsuma agar has **D-mannitol as carbon source, 7% NaCl, and human group O or rabbit blood**.

- Without optimal salt, most halophilic species don't grow. In broth they form a pellicle and uniform turbidity.

#### 4. Identification

**4a. Morphology.** Gram-negative rods, 1–3 by 0.5–0.8 µm, curved (comma) or straight, very motile, **polar monotrichous**, non-sporulating, non-capsulated.

**4b. Culture.** On TCBS, large yellow colonies of 2–4 mm (*V. cholerae*). On BSA, transparent S colonies "like dewdrops." In APW, a surface veil.

**4c. Biochemistry.**

**MAYA:** Preliminary tests first.

**ALEX:** There are two imperatives: **culture halophiles on media with optimal NaCl**, and **identify *V. cholerae* urgently**. The second is helped by the species being non-halophilic.

- **The oxidase test** is positive in vibrios. It separates *Vibrio*, *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas shigelloides* (oxidase +) from **enterobacteria** (oxidase –), such as ***Proteus vulgaris*, which also grows on TCBS**. Only *V. metschnikovii* is oxidase-negative.
  - **Careful: oxidase done on TCBS isolates can be falsely positive because of the sucrose, so do it on a non-selective medium.**
- **The O/129 vibriostatic test**: disks of **10 µg and 150 µg** on trypticase soy agar, inoculated as for disk-diffusion AST with a **0.5 McFarland** suspension. **Any zone of inhibition after overnight incubation means a positive test.**
- **Tolerance to 6–6.5% NaCl** correctly separates *Vibrio* (tolerant) from *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas*, which don't grow at that salinity.
- **The "string test" for DNA**: suspend a suspect colony in a drop of **0.5% sodium deoxycholate** on a slide. **Vibrios lyse and release their DNA, which can be "pulled" with a loop like a string.** This quickly separates *Vibrio* from *Aeromonas* (both oxidase-positive with similar colonies).
- **TSI screening**: medically relevant vibrios **ferment glucose without gas**, may or may not ferment sucrose, usually (diarrheagenic species) **don't ferment lactose and don't produce H₂S**. **Diarrheagenic aeromonads produce gas from glucose. *Proteus vulgaris* produces H₂S.**

**4d. Antigens.**
- **Slide agglutination with anti-cholera O:1 serum** on isolates with **yellow colonies on TCBS and a positive oxidase on non-selective medium**. Those that agglutinate are confirmed biochemically.
- **At the reference centre** the positive reaction is confirmed and the **serotype (Ogawa, Inaba, Hikojima)** identified. **If O:1 is negative, slide agglutination with anti-O:139 serum** is done.
- **Combined biovar + serovar** gives a rough epidemiological marker.

**4e.** Pathogenicity tests not routine; enterotoxigenicity and cytotoxicity can be evaluated experimentally.

**4f. Virulence.**
- **Toxigenicity of *V. cholerae* by VET-RPLA** (reversed passive latex agglutination).
- **ELISA** for characteristic *V. cholerae* structures.
- *Modern layer*: PCR for ***ctxA/ctxB*** (cholera toxin), ***tcpA*** (toxin-coregulated pilus), ***toxR***, ***ompW***, and for ***tdh/trh*** in *V. parahaemolyticus*.

**4g. Phage typing.**
- Reserved for **reference labs**. Some systems define **over 140 phage types**.
- **El Tor biovar phage typing** was greatly improved by **adding 5 new phages to the Basu-Mukerjee set of 5 lytic phages (I–V)**, and by redefining the test dilution from confluent lysis to **near-confluent lysis**. That defined **146 lysovars, covering typing of about 99% of strains**. Discriminatory power is excellent, except for **lysovar 115 (prevalence 12%)**.

**4h. Other typing.**
- **Serotyping (Ogawa/Inaba/Hikojima) and biotyping (classical/El Tor)** are the basic epidemiological markers.
- **Molecular methods** are currently harder to access, but they give reliable markers for El Tor.

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- **Ribotyping and PCR** at reference centres (mentioned in the course).
- *Modern layer*: **MALDI-TOF**; **WGS** to track the **pandemic lineages (7PET)** and the **SXT resistance islands**; **rapid immunochromatographic dipstick tests for O1/O139 antigen**, used as **field screening in epidemics (WHO)**.

#### 5. Susceptibility testing

**ALEX:**
- **Standardized disk diffusion**, especially to **monitor the emergence of resistant strains**.
- *Modern layer*: **EUCAST has specific criteria for *V. cholerae***. Test tetracycline/doxycycline, azithromycin, ciprofloxacin and cotrimoxazole.
- **Tetracycline resistance is plasmid-controlled** (mentioned in the course too). **The integrative conjugative element SXT carries resistance to cotrimoxazole, streptomycin and chloramphenicol.**

### Serology

**ALEX:**
- **Not used in routine diagnosis.** After cholera there's immunity to reinfection, but its duration and degree are unknown.
- **Gastric acidity gives some protection** when a small number of vibrios is swallowed.
- In experimental animals **specific IgA antibodies appear in the gut lumen**. **Antibodies against the O antigen protect lab animals**, and similar antibodies appear in human serum after infection, somewhat associated with protection against colonization.
- **The presence of antitoxin antibodies is NOT associated with protection.**

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **The essential role belongs to restoring fluid and electrolyte balance**: rehydration with electrolyte solutions, **first parenteral**, then **oral once the gut tolerates it**.
- **Oral tetracycline reduces the diarrhea and shortens vibrio excretion.** In some endemic areas (plasmid) resistance has appeared. Alternatives: **aminoglycosides or chloramphenicol**, until the antibiogram is back.

**MAYA:** Epidemiology.

**ALEX:**
- Transmission is through **food and water contaminated with feces**.
- In endemic areas **asymptomatic carriers** play an important role, **but not through person-to-person contact**.
- **After floods and disasters the epidemic risk rises substantially.**
- **7 pandemics have been reported since 1817.** **All except the 7th started in the Ganges delta.**
- **Romania hasn't had cholera cases since 1995.**

## PART B — *AEROMONAS* AND *PLESIOMONAS*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:**
- ***Aeromonas***: taxonomy under revision. The classic species of the **psychrophilic group** (*A. salmonicida* and its subspecies) and the **mesophilic group** (*A. hydrophila, A. caviae, A. sobria*) are really **phenospecies**. DNA–DNA hybridization defined **12 hybridization groups (HG)**, which rank as **genomospecies**:
  - HG1 *A. hydrophila*, HG3 *A. salmonicida*, **HG4 *A. caviae***, HG5 *A. media*, HG6 *A. eucrenophila*, HG7 *A. sobria*, **HG8 *A. veronii* biovar *sobria***, HG9 *A. jandaei*, HG10 *A. veronii* biovar *veronii*, HG12 *A. schubertii*. HG2 and HG11 remained unnamed.
  - 16S rRNA sequencing added *A. allosaccharophila* and *A. trota*.
  - Sequence analysis justifies **family rank** for aeromonads, ***Aeromonadaceae***.
- ***Plesiomonas***: **one species, *P. shigelloides***.
- *Modern layer*: ***Plesiomonas shigelloides* is now classified in *Enterobacteriaceae*/order Enterobacterales.** **It's the only oxidase-positive species in that order.**

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- ***Aeromonas***: commonly isolated from **fresh water** (rivers, lakes, wells, water tanks), where it **multiplies in the warm season**. Also found in the gut contents of fish, amphibians, reptiles and some higher vertebrates. It easily contaminates soil and farm produce. In marine biotopes it's only occasional.
- ***Plesiomonas shigelloides***: often isolated from **fresh and estuarine water with fecal pollution**. Concentrations depend on ambient temperature, high in summer and low in winter. Isolated from estuarine sediments, eels, fish, amphibians, crabs and shellfish. Although it lives in the gut of many warm-blooded animals (pigs, cats, dogs, poultry), **only under crowded, unhygienic conditions can it transiently colonize children's intestines**.

### General characters

**ALEX:**

***Aeromonas*:**
- Gram-negative rods, sometimes coccoid, **0.3–1 by 1–3.5 µm**, straight, **indistinguishable from coliforms**.
- **Motile by a single polar flagellum (monotrichous)**. In young cultures on agar they can become **peritrichous**.
- **All *A. salmonicida* subspecies are non-flagellated and non-motile.**
- Undemanding, **catalase- and oxidase-positive**, facultatively anaerobic. They attack sugars **fermentatively, with or without gas**, and **reduce nitrates to nitrites**.
- **Resistant to O/129.**
- They grow without added salt and are **inhibited at 6% NaCl**. They tolerate up to **4% NaCl, pH 5–9, and bile salts**.

***Plesiomonas shigelloides*:**
- Gram-negative rod, **0.8–1 by 3.0 µm**, indistinguishable from enterobacteria.
- **Motile by a single polar flagellum.** In wet mounts it shows **tumbling movements**, different from the oscillating motility of peritrichous enterobacteria.
- Undemanding, facultatively anaerobic, **catalase- and oxidase-positive**.
- Ferments **only a few carbohydrates, without gas**. **Reduces nitrates.**
- Grows on all routine media over **wide temperature (8–45 °C) and pH (4.5–8.5) ranges**. **Needs no added salt, tolerates 5% NaCl, but not 6%.**

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- ***Aeromonas***: **O serogroups and ribotyping** are the epidemiological markers, available **only in reference labs**.
- ***Plesiomonas shigelloides*** **agglutinates well with anti-*Shigella sonnei* serum**, a possible source of confusion in preliminary testing.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- ***Aeromonas***: gastroenteritis is caused by **two enterotoxins**, a **β-hemolytic cytolysin (aerolysin)** and **a cytotoxin**. There are several aerolysins that are immunologically and genetically distinct but share a single mechanism. The aerolysins of *A. hydrophila* and *A. veronii* are the most closely related immunologically. In mice, ***A. hydrophila* and *A. sobria* seem more virulent than *A. caviae***.
- ***Plesiomonas***: pathogenicity factors poorly defined.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- ***Aeromonas***: of 14 species, **at least 9, all mesophilic**, have been implicated in human infection. **Gastroenteritis.** Extra-digestive infections: **wound infections**, bacteriuria and, in the **immunocompromised host** (aplastic anemia, leukemia, sarcoma, **liver cirrhosis**), systemic infections.
  - **The pathogenicity score (Buiuc 31-33)**:
    - **1 = major human pathogen**: ***A. hydrophila, A. caviae, A. veronii* biovar *sobria***;
    - **2 = less frequent isolation, rarely proven etiological role**: *A. jandaei, A. schubertii*;
    - **3 = rare isolation, unknown clinical significance**: *A. veronii* biovar *veronii*, *A. trota*.
- ***Plesiomonas shigelloides***: occasional **acute diarrhea**, rarely isolated from blood cultures or CSF.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1.** Feces, wound specimens, urine, blood; food and water samples (investigations).
**2.** Nonspecific; wet mount from feces.

**3. Culture.**

***Aeromonas*:**
- Undemanding. It grows on the usual selective media for enterobacteria or vibrios. **Because it ferments lactose or sucrose (often both), it can easily be mistaken for non-pathogenic coliforms (and so missed) or for vibrios.**
- **Medium of choice: blood agar with cefsulodin-irgasan-novobiocin (CIN)**, sold commercially. **Ampicillin (5 µg/ml)** is another selective agent.
- **Mesophiles** grow best at 35–37 °C, forming **3–5 mm grey, slightly rough colonies overnight**.
- ***A. caviae* colonies are non-hemolytic.** Many strains of other species are **β-hemolytic on sheep blood agar** and could be **mistaken for *P. aeruginosa* on a quick look**. But they have **neither the metallic sheen nor the characteristic aroma** of the pyocyanic bacillus. Some strains show **double hemolysis** (outer incomplete zone, inner complete zone).
- ***A. salmonicida***, a psychrophilic fish pathogen, grows best at **22–25 °C**. On media with **1% tyrosine** it forms a **water-soluble brown pigment**. Among mesophiles, only some *A. media* and HG2 strains make pigment on heart infusion agar.

***Plesiomonas*:**
- **Inhibited and grows very poorly** on moderately selective media for vibrios. It **grows well on MacConkey**, forming **colonies that look like shigellae**.
- On blood agar after 24 h at 37 °C: **round, convex, grey, non-hemolytic colonies of 1–2 mm**.

**4a.** *Aeromonas*: polar monotrichous rods or coccobacilli. *Plesiomonas*: rod with a single polar flagellum, indistinguishable from enterobacteria.
**4b.** *Aeromonas*: 3–5 mm grey, slightly rough, often β-hemolytic colonies. *Plesiomonas*: 1–2 mm convex grey non-hemolytic colonies, *Shigella*-like on MacConkey.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- **The key test is a positive oxidase**, which separates them from enterobacteria in preliminary screening. *Aeromonas* strains can otherwise be confused with ***Escherichia*, *Enterobacter* or *Providencia***.
- **Versus *Vibrio***, use the minimal tests from Table 31-30: ***Aeromonas* is resistant to O/129 and ampicillin, doesn't grow at 6% NaCl, and is string-test negative.** ***Plesiomonas* is O/129-sensitive, doesn't grow at 6% NaCl, and makes no gas.**
- ***Aeromonas* species identification (Buiuc 31-33)**: indole, Moeller decarboxylases (arginine, lysine, ornithine), gas from glucose, VP, acid from L-arabinose, lactose, sucrose, m-inositol, D-mannitol, salicin, esculin hydrolysis, β-hemolysis on sheep blood, and resistance to ampicillin 10 µg. Landmarks:
  - ***A. hydrophila***: esculin +, VP +, arabinose +, hemolysis +;
  - ***A. caviae***: hemolysis –, ornithine –, VP +;
  - ***A. veronii* biovar *sobria***: esculin –, arabinose –, hemolysis +;
  - ***A. schubertii***: indole –, sucrose –, mannitol –;
  - ***A. trota***: **ampicillin-susceptible**, the exception to the genus's resistance.
- Extra tests for correct differentiation: DL-lactate and urocanic acid utilization, D-rhamnose fermentation (*A. hydrophila* / HG2–*A. salmonicida*); DL-lactate, mannose fermentation, pyrazinamidase and β-hemolysis (*A. caviae* / *A. media*). These are **reference-lab work**. **Errors for clinically significant isolates don't matter much**, given the pathogenicity difference between *A. hydrophila* and *A. caviae*. ***A. allosaccharophila* can't be correctly identified in a clinical lab.**
- ***Plesiomonas***: confirmed by the tests in Table 31-33 and separated from vibrios and aeromonads by Table 31-30. **Isolation and identification pose no problems.**

**4d.** *Aeromonas*: O serogroups, reference labs only. *Plesiomonas*: **watch for cross-agglutination with anti-*Shigella sonnei* serum.**
**4e.** Not routine.
**4f.** *Modern*: PCR for ***aerA*** (aerolysin), *hlyA*, *act*.
**4g.** No widely used phage typing schemes.
**4h.** **O serogroups**, epidemiological markers available only to reference labs.
**4i.** **Ribotyping** is retained as an epidemiological marker for *Aeromonas*. *Modern*: MALDI-TOF (with updated databases it separates the genomospecies well), *rpoD/gyrB* sequencing, WGS.

**5. Susceptibility.**
- Disk diffusion.
- ***Aeromonas***: **natural resistance to ampicillin (except *A. trota*) and amoxicillin-clavulanate**. It often has **multiple inducible chromosomal β-lactamases**: a penicillinase, an **AmpC** cephalosporinase and the **metallo-β-lactamase carbapenemase CphA**. **Avoid β-lactam monotherapy in severe infections.** Active drugs: fluoroquinolones, cotrimoxazole, aminoglycosides, third-generation cephalosporins.
- ***Plesiomonas***: natural resistance to aminopenicillins; susceptible to cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins.

### Serology / Treatment

**ALEX:** No serology. **Gastroenteritis is usually self-limiting.** Severe forms and extraintestinal infections are treated according to the antibiogram.

## PART A — *CAMPYLOBACTER*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** *Campylobacter* organisms were **once grouped with the vibrios**. They were known as animal pathogens causing septicemia, abortion or enteritis, and in **1963** they were called **"microaerophilic vibrios."**

**MAYA:** Then human disease was recognized.

**ALEX:** Yes. ***Campylobacter jejuni*** was identified as a **cause of human diarrhea**. Based on **16S rRNA sequencing**, these "vibrios" were placed in the genera ***Campylobacter*** (18 species and subspecies), ***Arcobacter***, ***Helicobacter*** and ***Wollinella***.

**MAYA:** The medically important species, from course Table 4.

**ALEX:**

| Species | Reservoir | Human disease |
|---|---|---|
| ***C. jejuni*** | animals and birds | **diarrhea** |
| ***C. fetus*** | cattle and sheep | **septicemia in immunocompromised patients** |
| ***C. coli*** | **pigs** | **diarrhea** |
| *C. hyointestinalis*, *C. fennelliae* | animals and birds | infections in homosexual men |

Other current species: *C. upsaliensis, C. lari, C. concisus*. ***C. jejuni* and *C. coli* together cause over 95% of human campylobacteriosis.** And ***C. jejuni* is the most frequently reported bacterial cause of diarrheal disease in the EU** (ECDC).

### Habitat

**ALEX:** The **intestinal tract of birds**, whose body temperature of about **42 °C is optimal for *C. jejuni***, and of mammals. Sources: **raw poultry**, **unpasteurized milk**, contaminated water, pets.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Thin, spiral, curved Gram-negative rods** (0.2–0.5 by 0.5–5 µm), shaped like a **comma, an "S", "gull wings"** or a spiral, especially in young cultures. In **old cultures they become coccoid**, degenerate and non-culturable forms. Non-sporulating.
- **Motile by a single polar flagellum** (mono- or amphitrichous), with a characteristic **"darting motility,"** described in Romanian as "midge flight." It's visible on a wet mount under dark field or phase contrast.
- **Microaerophilic and capnophilic**: they need an atmosphere of about **5% O₂, 10% CO₂ and 85% N₂**.
- **They neither ferment nor oxidize carbohydrates.** Their metabolism is based on amino acids and Krebs cycle intermediates, **so classical biochemical tests are useless**.
- **Catalase +** (*C. jejuni, C. coli, C. fetus*), **oxidase +**, **nitrate reduction +**.

**MAYA:** And the temperature trick.

**ALEX:** **Thermophily.** ***C. jejuni, C. coli* and *C. lari* grow at 42 °C and do NOT grow at 25 °C.** That's an essential isolation trait. ***C. fetus* grows at 25 °C, but not at 42 °C.**

- **Sensitive to drying, acidity and atmospheric oxygen.** They're killed by pasteurization and by cooking meat.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:**
- **O antigen**, an LPS with lipooligosaccharide (LOS) structure. **H flagellar antigen** (flagellin FlaA/FlaB). **Capsular polysaccharide (CPS)**, the basis of the **Penner serotyping scheme (HS, heat-stable)**. The **Lior scheme (HL, heat-labile)** uses heat-labile surface antigens.
- **Molecular mimicry.** The LOS of some serotypes, **especially HS:19 and HS:41**, mimics the **gangliosides GM1/GD1a of peripheral nerves**. That's the basis of **post-infectious Guillain-Barré syndrome**.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **Motility (the flagellum) and spiral shape**, which let it penetrate the intestinal mucus layer.
- **Adhesins**: **CadF** (binds fibronectin), JlpA, PEB1. It invades enterocytes and multiplies intracellularly.
- **Cytolethal distending toxin (CDT)**, which **blocks the cell cycle at G2/M**.
- **LOS** with endotoxin activity and a role in molecular mimicry.
- **Low infective dose: 500–800 organisms.**

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- **Campylobacter enteritis.** **Incubation 2–5 days.** A **flu-like prodrome** (fever, headache, myalgia), then **intense periumbilical or right iliac fossa pain ("pseudo-appendicitis")** and diarrhea, **often muco-bloody**. **Self-limiting in 5–7 days.**
- **Post-infectious complications:**
  - **Guillain-Barré syndrome** (and its **Miller-Fisher** variant). ***C. jejuni* is the most frequently identified infectious trigger**;
  - **reactive arthritis**;
  - **post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome**;
  - **erythema nodosum**.
- ***C. fetus*** has **vascular tropism**: **bacteremia, endocarditis, thrombophlebitis, meningitis and septic abortion**, especially in the **immunocompromised, cirrhotic, diabetic and elderly**. Its **surface S protein protects it from serum bactericidal activity**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.** **Feces collected early, in the acute phase.** Use **Cary-Blair** transport medium, at **+4 °C if transport takes more than 2 hours**. For ***C. fetus***, **blood culture**. It grows in standard bottles, but **slowly, over 5–14 days**.

**2. Macro and micro.**
- Macroscopically: diarrheal stool, **often with mucus and blood**.
- Microscopically: a **wet mount** shows **numerous leukocytes and red cells** (invasive mechanism) and bacteria with **"darting" motility** on dark field or phase contrast. **A Gram smear of stool**, with prolonged staining or **basic fuchsin counterstain** (standard Gram stains campylobacters poorly), can show **"gull-wing"** spiral forms. It's a rapid test with **good specificity but modest sensitivity**.

**3. Culture.**
- **Selective media, all containing antibiotics** to inhibit fecal flora:
  - **Skirrow** (vancomycin, polymyxin B, trimethoprim);
  - **Butzler**;
  - **Preston**;
  - **Campy-BAP (Blaser)**;
  - **CCDA** (charcoal-cefoperazone-deoxycholate, **blood-free**, the **most used today**);
  - **Karmali**.
- **Essential incubation conditions: microaerophilic atmosphere (5% O₂, 10% CO₂, 85% N₂), at 42 °C, for 48 hours.** The thermophily selects *C. jejuni/C. coli*. For ***C. fetus***: **37 °C or 25 °C, on media without cephalosporins**.

**MAYA:** The filtration technique.

**ALEX:** **The Cape Town method.** A fecal suspension is **filtered through 0.45–0.65 µm membranes laid on a non-selective medium**. **Campylobacters are small and very motile, so they pass through the filter, but the accompanying flora doesn't.** It's useful for **cephalosporin-sensitive species**.

- **Colonies**: **flat, grey, moist and shiny, tending to spread along the inoculation line**, sometimes with a metallic sheen. **Non-hemolytic.**

**4a.** Thin, spiral or curved Gram-negative rods: "gull wings," "S," commas. Coccoid in old cultures. Motile with a single polar flagellum.
**4b.** Flat, grey, moist colonies spread along the streaks. **Growth only under microaerophilic conditions.** *C. jejuni/C. coli* grow at 42 °C, not at 25 °C.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- **Classical biochemical tests are useless** because the organisms are asaccharolytic. The tests used are **oxidase +, catalase +, nitrate reduction +**.
- **Sodium hippurate hydrolysis is the key test**: **positive in *C. jejuni*, negative in *C. coli***. It's **the only simple phenotypic test that separates them.**
- **Indoxyl acetate hydrolysis**: positive in *C. jejuni* and *C. coli*, **negative in *C. lari***.
- **Disk susceptibility**: ***C. jejuni/C. coli* are sensitive to nalidixic acid and resistant to cephalothin**. ***C. fetus* is the reverse**: resistant to nalidixic acid and sensitive to cephalothin. It's a classic test, but **of limited value today because of acquired quinolone resistance**.
- Growth at 25 / 37 / 42 °C; growth with **1% glycine and 3.5% NaCl**; H₂S on TSI.

**4d. Antigens.** **Penner serotyping (HS, heat-stable, over 60 serotypes) and Lior (HL, heat-labile)**, reserved for reference labs. **Serotype HS:19 is associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome.**

**4e.** Not routine.
**4f.** PCR for ***cdtB*** (CDT), ***cadF***, ***flaA***; detection of the LOS classes associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome.
**4g.** Phage typing schemes exist, reserved for reference labs.
**4h.** Biotyping (**Lior scheme**), resistotyping, ***flaA*-RFLP**, **PFGE**, and **MLST (the Dingle scheme, 7 housekeeping genes), the epidemiological standard for *C. jejuni***.

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- **Multiplex PCR directly from stool** (syndromic GI panels). It's **more sensitive than culture**, but **gives no isolate for the antibiogram**.
- **Rapid immunochromatographic tests for *Campylobacter* antigen in stool**, used for screening, with **mandatory confirmation by culture**.
- **MALDI-TOF**: fast, reliable species identification from colonies. It **has replaced the hippurate test in many labs**.
- **WGS/cgMLST** for outbreak and food-source investigation. Sequencing of ***gyrA* and the 23S rRNA gene** for resistance.

**5. Susceptibility testing.**
- **EUCAST disk diffusion on Mueller-Hinton with blood, under microaerophilic conditions, at 41 °C for 24 hours.** Test **erythromycin, ciprofloxacin and tetracycline**.
- **Natural resistance**: trimethoprim, sulfonamides, vancomycin, rifampicin, colistin (some species), aztreonam.
- **Acquired resistance to fluoroquinolones is very common**, from the **Thr-86-Ile mutation in *gyrA***, selected by quinolone use in animal farming. **In some countries it's over 50% of isolates.** **Macrolide resistance** (23S rRNA mutations, *erm(B)* methylase) **remains low**.

### Serology

**ALEX:** **Not used in routine diagnosis of enteritis.** Serology (**ELISA for IgG/IgA anti-*C. jejuni***) has value **only retrospectively**, when investigating the cause of **Guillain-Barré syndrome and reactive arthritis**, and in epidemiological studies.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Enteritis is usually self-limiting.** Treat with fluid and electrolyte rebalancing. **Antibiotics are used only in severe or prolonged forms, in the immunocompromised, in pregnancy or with bacteremia**: **azithromycin or erythromycin (drugs of choice)**, and **ciprofloxacin only if the antibiogram confirms susceptibility**.
- **Systemic *C. fetus***: **gentamicin, ampicillin or carbapenems**. **Macrolides are not active.**
- **Prevention**: proper cooking of poultry, avoiding cross-contamination in the kitchen, milk pasteurization, drinking-water control, and biosecurity on poultry farms.

## PART B — *HELICOBACTER PYLORI*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** **The first culture of *H. pylori* was obtained in 1982 by Warren and Marshall.** The organisms isolated from patients with gastritis and duodenal ulcer were first called ***Campylobacter*-like**, then ***Campylobacter pyloridis***, then ***Campylobacter pylori***. **Since 1989 they belong to the new genus *Helicobacter*, with *H. pylori* as type species.**

- It was classified by **16S rRNA sequencing**, alongside *Campylobacter, Arcobacter* and *Wollinella*.
- **Warren and Marshall received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005.**

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- It colonizes the stomach in **60–70% of non-ulcer dyspepsias**, and **much more often in peptic ulcer**.
- It's found at the **luminal pole of gastric epithelial cells**, **without invading the gastric mucosa**, mainly in the **antrum**.
- **Urea is an important nutrient**, and **ammonia produced by urease gives it the optimal pH** for multiplication.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- It looks like a **Gram-negative vibrio**, **2.5 µm long and 0.5 µm wide**, with **one or more polar flagella**. It's **motile with characteristic movements**, sometimes spiral-shaped, non-capsulated and non-sporulating. It may also have lateral cilia.
- **It doesn't reduce nitrates and doesn't hydrolyze sodium hippurate.** Some strains produce **H₂S on TSI**. It makes **urease and catalase**.
- **Microaerophilic and oxidase-positive.** **Strong urease is the defining biochemical trait.**
- **Very susceptible to drying.** It survives relatively poorly outside the host, but **can survive and multiply on the gastric mucosa**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** Its structural components define the antigens: a **somatic O antigen (heat-stable)**, a **flagellar H antigen (heat-labile)**, and a **colonization (adherence) antigen**.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **The surface (colonization, adherence) antigen** allows attachment to the gastric epithelium, followed by multiplication and colony formation.
- **Urease**: it releases ammonia from urea and **neutralizes gastric acidity**. Together with **flagella (motility)**, it plays an important role in pathogenicity.
- **Somatic O antigen** (endotoxin, LPS): a direct and indirect role in pathogenesis.
- **A cytotoxic toxin** that starts local inflammation by acting on gastric and duodenal epithelial cells.
- **A toxin that inhibits HCl secretion** by the gastric parietal cells.
- *Modern nomenclature*:
  - **VacA**, the **vacuolating cytotoxin**. **Alleles s1/m1 are more virulent**;
  - **the *cag* pathogenicity island with the CagA protein**, **injected into the epithelial cell by a type IV secretion system**. **CagA-positive strains carry a higher risk of ulcer and gastric adenocarcinoma**;
  - **adhesin BabA**, which binds the **Lewis b** antigen.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- **Gastritis** (acute and chronic active) and **peptic ulcer** (gastric and duodenal). **Infection is a risk factor for gastric cancer.**
- *Modern layer*: **WHO/IARC classifies *H. pylori* as a group I carcinogen.** It's also linked to **gastric MALT lymphoma**, which **can regress after eradication**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.**
- **Collecting and transporting the specimen is an absolutely essential step.**
- Specimen: **biopsies of the gastric or duodenal mucosa**, obtained by **upper digestive endoscopy**. **Multiple biopsies from antrum and body are preferred**, because colonization is **patchy**.
- **Fast transport, in saline or a specific medium (Stuart, Portagerm pylori), at +4 °C, with processing within 2–4 hours.**
- **Non-invasive specimens**: **exhaled breath** (urea breath test), **feces** (stool antigen), **serum** (serology), and **gastric juice**.

**2. Macro and micro.**
- **Endoscopy** made it possible to examine the digestive mucosa and clarified normal and abnormal appearances: **erythema, edema, exudate, hemorrhagic petechiae, ulcerations, scars**.
- **Biopsies are crushed onto a slide (touch preparations, or imprints)**. They're **fixed with methyl alcohol for 1 minute**, dried, and **stained for 20 minutes with 1/10 Giemsa solution, freshly prepared**. The **characteristic vibrio or spirillum morphology** appears.
- *Modern layer*: **histopathology** on sections (modified Giemsa, **Warthin-Starry silver stain**, immunohistochemistry) is the **reference standard**. It also lets you assess gastritis, atrophy and metaplasia at the same time.

**3. Culture.**
- It grows in **2–7 days** on **non-selective media** (chocolate agar, brain-heart infusion media, **Brucella agar with 5–7% horse blood**) or on **selective media** with added **amphotericin B and polymyxin, or cefsulodin**, for example.
- Conditions: **relatively high humidity and 5–10% CO₂** (microaerophilic atmosphere).
- *Modern layer*: small (0.5–1 mm), translucent, non-hemolytic colonies. **Culture has modest sensitivity, but it's the only method that allows an antibiogram.**

**4a.** Gram-negative vibrio or spirillum, 2.5 by 0.5 µm, one or more polar flagella, motile with characteristic movements; non-capsulated, non-sporulating; may have lateral cilia.
**4b.** Slow growth (2–7 days), only in a **moist microaerophilic atmosphere**, on blood-enriched media.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- **Urease + (very strong), catalase +.** Doesn't reduce nitrates or hydrolyze hippurate. Some strains make H₂S on TSI. **Oxidase +.**

**MAYA:** The urease test.

**ALEX:** It can be done **two ways**:
- **colorimetrically**, by adding **phenolphthalein**, which **turns red** when urea is broken down;
- or with **urea labeled with carbon atoms (¹³C or ¹⁴C)**, then measuring the **CO₂** released when urease splits the urea.

*Modern layer*, in practice:
- **The rapid urease test (RUT/CLO-test)** on the biopsy: results in **1–24 hours**, cheap, with good sensitivity and specificity.
- **The urea breath test (UBT)**: **the non-invasive method of choice** for diagnosis and, **above all, for confirming eradication**. It's done **at least 4 weeks after treatment and 2 weeks after stopping proton pump inhibitors**.

**4d. Antigens.**
- After culture, identification can use morphological, cultural, biochemical and antigenic characters.
- *Modern*: **the monoclonal stool antigen test** (ELISA or immunochromatographic). It's a validated non-invasive method, **useful both for diagnosis and for confirming eradication**.

**4e.** Not done.
**4f.** PCR for ***ureA, glmM, cagA, vacA*** (s1/s2, m1/m2 genotyping), from biopsy, gastric juice or feces.
**4g.** Not done.
**4h.** ***cagA/vacA*** genotyping; RAPD, PFGE and MLST for molecular epidemiology and **intrafamilial transmission** studies.

**4i. New-generation methods.**
- **Real-time PCR with simultaneous detection of resistance mutations**: **clarithromycin** (**A2142G, A2143G in the 23S rRNA gene**) and **levofloxacin** (*gyrA*), directly from biopsy or stool. This is the **current strategy recommended by the Maastricht guidelines** for **susceptibility-guided treatment**.
- MALDI-TOF from colonies. **WGS for population studies.** ***H. pylori* phylogeography mirrors human migrations.**

**5. Susceptibility testing.**
- **Gradient diffusion (E-test) on Mueller-Hinton with blood, microaerophilic, 72 hours**, for **clarithromycin, levofloxacin, metronidazole, amoxicillin, tetracycline and rifabutin**.
- The molecular alternative is used more and more, since the organism is hard to culture.
- **Clarithromycin resistance (over 15% in many European regions, including Romania) contraindicates standard clarithromycin triple therapy without prior testing.**

### Serology

**ALEX:**
- **ELISA serology can help establish the diagnosis.**
- **Immune response**: infected patients make **specific IgM**, plus **IgG and IgA**, which persist **systemically and in the gastric mucosa**, with **higher titers in chronic infection**.
- *Modern*: **the key limitation is that serology can't tell active infection from a cured one**, because antibodies persist for months or years after eradication. **So it's not used to confirm eradication.** It's useful in epidemiological studies and in patients with **GI bleeding, atrophy, or recent PPI or antibiotic treatment**, where the other tests give false negatives.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- From the course: therapy can use **bismuth preparations**, **H₂-receptor blockers** (cimetidine, famotidine), **omeprazole or lansoprazole** (which also have **bactericidal action against *Helicobacter***), plus antibiotics such as **metronidazole**, following well-established schemes.
- *Modern* (**Maastricht VI**):
  - **bismuth quadruple therapy** (PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole), or
  - **non-bismuth (concomitant) quadruple therapy**, PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin + metronidazole, **14 days**;
  - **clarithromycin triple therapy only where local resistance is below 15%**;
  - **confirm eradication at least 4 weeks later, by UBT or stool antigen**.

**MAYA:** Epidemiology.

**ALEX:**
- **Prevalence rises with age**: **under 20% in people under 30**, and **40–60% at 60 years**, including asymptomatic people. **In developing countries it can reach 80% of adults.**
- The infection is seen as a feature of **disadvantaged socio-economic settings**.
- **Transmission seems to be fecal-oral and oral-oral**, helped by close contact within the family.

## PART A — THE GENERAL FRAME

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:** "Non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria" is a **huge, artificially built grouping**, put together for the **practical needs of preliminary identification** in clinical microbiology. The classic chapter covers **only nutritionally undemanding taxa**. The demanding genera (*Neisseria, Brucella, Bordetella, Kingella*) belong in other chapters.

**MAYA:** Who's in it?

**ALEX:**

- **Gram-negative coccobacilli**: *Acinetobacter, Moraxella*. Buiuc treats these separately, in chapter 32.2.

**MAYA:** The five rRNA homology groups, Table 32-1.

**ALEX:**
- **Group I, *Pseudomonadaceae***: genus *Pseudomonas*, with **fluorescent** species (*P. aeruginosa, P. putida, P. fluorescens*) and **non-fluorescent** ones (*P. alcaligenes, P. pseudoalcaligenes, P. mendocina, P. stutzeri*).
- **Group II, *Burkholderiaceae***: *B. mallei, B. pseudomallei, B. cepacia, B. gladioli*, and the genus *Ralstonia* with *R. pickettii*.
- **Group III, *Comamonadaceae***: *Comamonas, Acidovorax, Delftia acidovorans*.
- **Group IV, *Caulobacteraceae***: *Brevundimonas diminuta, B. vesicularis*.
- **Group V, *Xanthomonadaceae***: ***Stenotrophomonas maltophilia***, CDC group I.
- **Unassigned**: *Chryseomonas luteola, Flavimonas oryzihabitans, Sphingomonas paucimobilis, Shewanella putrefaciens*.

**MAYA:** And the renamings.

**ALEX:**
- *Pseudomonas maltophilia* → *Xanthomonas maltophilia* → ***Stenotrophomonas maltophilia***;
- *Burkholderia picketii* → ***Ralstonia pickettii***;
- *Comamonas acidovorans* → ***Delftia acidovorans***;
- *Ralstonia paucula* → ***Wautersia paucula***;
- *Pseudomonas putrefaciens* → ***Shewanella putrefaciens***.

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- Pseudomonads can use a **wide variety of organic compounds as their only carbon and energy source**, even at minimal concentrations. They grow at neutral pH from **a few degrees above freezing up to 42 °C**. That explains how widespread they are: surface water, moist subsoil, plants and food.
- **They're well represented in the hospital microbial background**: distilled water, saline, **dye solutions, antiseptic and disinfectant solutions**, irrigation fluids, medicines (including **repeatedly used antibiotic solutions**), infusion fluids, **blood for transfusion**, eye drops, instruments, plumbing, **ornamental plants and flower vases**.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Fine Gram-negative rods**, **0.3–0.5 µm wide by 1.5–3 µm long**, single, in pairs or short chains. **The Gram stain doesn't distinguish among non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli.**
- **Motility**:
  - pseudomonads (*Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, Brevundimonas, Acidovorax, Comamonas, Delftia, Chryseomonas, Flavimonas, Shewanella, Stenotrophomonas*) are **motile by polar flagella, mono- or lophotrichous**;
  - ***Sphingomonas paucimobilis* is non-motile at 37 °C and motile at 18–22 °C**;
  - **exception: *Burkholderia mallei* is a non-motile, non-flagellated species**;
  - other genera are **peritrichous** (*Rhizobium, Alcaligenes, Ochrobactrum*), have **one polar flagellum** (*Balneatrix*), or are **non-motile** (*Flavobacterium, Myroides, Weeksella, Bergeyella*).
- **Aerobic, with oxidative metabolism, NEVER fermentative.** **Only nitrate respiration allows anaerobic growth.** Saccharolytic or asaccharolytic. **Catalase-positive and most often oxidase-positive.** Most species reduce nitrates, either to nitrite or to nitrogen gas. **G+C content 57–70 mol%.**

**MAYA:** And the key test to separate them from enterobacteria.

**ALEX:** **Oxidation (not fermentation) of glucose on Hugh-Leifson OF (oxidation/fermentation) medium**, plus **the oxidase test**.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** Serotypes are described within species based on several O and H factors. They're useful mainly for epidemiological investigations.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **Virulence is generally low.** They're conditional or accidental pathogens, **except *B. mallei* and *B. pseudomallei***.
- **Risk conditions** for non-fermenter infections: **wounds and burns, hematological malignancies and other immunosuppression, intensive care** (prolonged and repeated catheterization, **mechanical ventilation**), especially when combined with or preceded by antibiotic therapy. ***Cystic fibrosis* is a major risk condition** for respiratory colonization or infection with *Pseudomonas* spp. and *B. cepacia*.
- Because virulence is low, **definitive identification must be well justified by clinical significance**. You have to tell **colonization from infection**, and **pseudobacteremia from bacteremia**.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:** From Table 32-2: **wound and burn infections, bacteremia and septicemia, endocarditis, catheter infections** (intravenous, peritoneal), **bronchopulmonary, ear, meningeal, eye, bone (osteomyelitis) and urinary infections**, plus **glanders (morva)** and **melioidosis**. At the top: ***P. aeruginosa*** (++++ in almost every category), ***Stenotrophomonas maltophilia***, ***B. cepacia***.

**MAYA:** Special situations.

**ALEX:**
- ***P. fluorescens*** causes **bacteremia and septic shock after transfusion**. It **colonizes the antecubital fossa in about 0.3% of blood donors** and **grows well in blood stored at 4 °C**.
- **"Swimmer's ear"** (*P. aeruginosa*) has a good prognosis. But **malignant otitis externa in diabetics**, without surgery and antibiotics, leads to fatal complications: **skull-base osteomyelitis and meningitis**.
- **Keratitis and corneal ulcers** progress rapidly to **perforation and endophthalmitis**.
- **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis** after repeated inhalation of aerosols contaminated with *P. fluorescens*.
- ***Chryseobacterium meningosepticum*** causes **infant meningitis**.
- ***Weeksella virosa / Bergeyella zoohelcum*** cause infection of **animal bite or scratch wounds**.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis: general

**1. Collection.** Depends on site: **pus from closed collections, CSF, joint fluid, peritoneal dialysis fluid**, urine, sputum or tracheal aspirate, blood, wound and burn swabs.

**2. Macro and micro.**
- Macroscopically: **pus that's blue or fluorescent yellow-green** suggests *P. aeruginosa*.
- Microscopically: Gram and methylene blue smears show fine Gram-negative rods. **The Gram stain doesn't separate species.**
- **Motility** is examined on a **wet mount** or, better, a **hanging drop** of an overnight broth culture, with the microscope light suitably stopped down. **Motility of these bacteria is NOT tested by stabbing into soft agar.** **The number and arrangement of flagella** are studied on **Leifson-stained** smears.

**3. Culture.**
- **All undemanding non-fermenters grow well on routine media, blood agar or chocolate agar**, used for isolation from **uncontaminated specimens** (pus from closed collections, CSF, joint fluid, peritoneal dialysis fluid).
- **Selective media are for contaminated specimens.** MacConkey, Istrati-Meitert and EMB have **low selectivity**.
- **More selective, for *P. aeruginosa***: **Pseudomonas base agar** with selective supplements: **Cetrimide (Merck), Irgasan (Difco), Cetrimide + Nalidixic acid**, or **Cetrimide + Fucidin + Cephaloridine** (Oxoid **C-N** and **C-F-C** media respectively). **The base agar stimulates synthesis of pyocyanin and pyoverdin.**
  - **On C-N agar *B. cepacia* is inhibited, *P. putida* partly inhibited, and *P. aeruginosa* and *P. fluorescens* grow very well.**
  - **On C-F-C agar both *Pseudomonas* and *Burkholderia* grow well.** Strains from the **respiratory secretions of cystic fibrosis patients are more sensitive to these selective agents**, so there's a **risk of false negatives**.
- **For *B. cepacia***:
  - **PC medium** (*P. cepacia*), with **polymyxin B** (Gilligan, 1985);
  - **OFPBL** (Oxidative-Fermentative base, Polymyxin B, Bacitracin, Lactose; Welch, 1987), moderately selective and differential, but it **doesn't separate *B. cepacia* from *B. gladioli***;
  - **BCSA** (*B. cepacia* selective agar), **shown to be better than both**, more sensitive and more specific (Henry, 1999).
- **For *B. pseudomallei***: **Ashdown medium**, selective through **crystal violet and gentamicin**, better than MacConkey even with added colistin.
- In **cystic fibrosis** patients, selective media are indicated to separate *P. aeruginosa*, *B. cepacia* and *S. maltophilia* in primary culture. **For suspected melioidosis**: sputum, throat swab, rectal swab.

**4c. Biochemistry: the four phenotypic identification groups.**

**ALEX:** The key characters (**von Graevenitz, 1995**) are **oxidative saccharolysis, oxidase production and indole production**. On that basis there are **4 main groups**:

1. **Saccharolytic, oxidase-positive, indole-negative** (Table 32-4): *Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, Ralstonia, Rhizobium radiobacter, Alcaligenes xylosoxidans* subsp. *xylosoxidans, Ochrobactrum anthropi, Flavobacterium* spp., *Pseudomonas*-like group 2, *Shewanella* spp., *Sphingomonas paucimobilis*.
2. **Asaccharolytic, oxidase-positive, indole-negative** (Table 32-5): *P. alcaligenes, P. pseudoalcaligenes, Alcaligenes* spp., *A. faecalis* type II, *Delftia acidovorans, Myroides odoratum/M. odoratimimus, Wautersia paucula*.
3. **Saccharolytic or asaccharolytic, oxidase-positive, indole-positive** (Table 32-6): ***Chryseobacterium meningosepticum***, *Flavobacterium* group IIb, *Empedobacter brevis*, CDC groups IIe, IIh and IIi, *Weeksella virosa, Bergeyella zoohelcum, Balneatrix alpica*.
4. **Oxidase-negative** (Table 32-7): *Chryseomonas luteola, Flavimonas oryzihabitans*, ***Stenotrophomonas maltophilia*** (and *Burkholderia mallei*, of which **about 75% of strains are oxidase-positive**).

**MAYA:** The bench technique.

**ALEX:**
- Inoculate **Hugh-Leifson medium** for the glucose oxidation/fermentation test and for acid from other carbohydrates. **In standard media with more peptone, alkaline metabolic products neutralize the acids from sugar oxidation.** Alternatively, an **ammonium-salt medium** can be used.
- **Brain-heart infusion agar** for growth at **4 °C and 42 °C**.
- **Thornley arginine broth** for **arginine dihydrolase**. **Moller medium is too anaerobic and can give false negatives with pseudomonads.**
- **The oxidase test is done after adding a drop of toluene** to the bacterial suspension, to speed up the color reaction.

**4d. Antigens.** **Serotyping** is an epidemiological marker, done in specialized labs.
**4e.** Not routine.
**4f.** PCR for virulence genes and resistance determinants.
**4g.** **Phage typing is useful for *P. aeruginosa***.
**4h. Other typing.**
- **Epidemiological markers**: **sero-, phage and pyocin typing** are useful for investigating ***P. aeruginosa*** infections, and **sero- and bacteriocin typing** for ***B. cepacia***.
- **Known biovars**: **5 biovars of *P. fluorescens*, 2 of *P. putida*, 3 of *R. pickettii*, 2 of *R. radiobacter*, and 2 of *S. putrefaciens***.
**4i. New-generation methods.**
- Molecular methods: **DNA analysis with restriction endonucleases, DNA probes, amplification of specific chromosomal sequences**. These are valuable epidemiological markers for *P. aeruginosa*. **Ribotyping** and others are used for *B. cepacia*.
- *Modern layer*: **MALDI-TOF is now the routine method for non-fermenters**, much better than biochemical galleries, **which perform poorly in this group**. **16S rRNA and *rpoB/recA* sequencing** is **indispensable for the *B. cepacia* complex**, which has **over 20 genomovars**.

**5. Susceptibility testing.**
- *Modern*: **specific EUCAST tables for *Pseudomonas* spp., *Acinetobacter* spp. and *Stenotrophomonas maltophilia***, with criteria different from enterobacteria.
- ***S. maltophilia***: **natural resistance to carbapenems (metallo-β-lactamase L1)** and most β-lactams, to aminoglycosides and to aztreonam. **The treatment of choice is cotrimoxazole**, with minocycline and levofloxacin as alternatives.
- **The *B. cepacia* complex**: natural resistance to **aminoglycosides, colistin**, aminopenicillins, and first- and second-generation cephalosporins. Active: **cotrimoxazole, meropenem, ceftazidime, minocycline**.

### Serology / Treatment

**ALEX:** **Not done.** Diagnosis is bacteriological, **except for melioidosis serology in endemic areas**. Treatment follows the antibiogram, often in combination.

## PART B — *PSEUDOMONAS AERUGINOSA*

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:**
- The family *Pseudomonadaceae* has four genera, and **only *Pseudomonas* contains medically important species**. Based on rRNA homology the genus had 5 groups. Some species once included now belong to *Burkholderia* (*B. mallei, B. pseudomallei*).
- **The most important species is *Pseudomonas aeruginosa***, the **"pyocyanic bacillus,"** or **"bacillus of blue pus."** It's **one of the main opportunistic bacteria**, often involved in infections in people with **low resistance** and in **nosocomial infections**.
- **In 1981 about 800 *Pseudomonas* species were thought to exist**, mostly contaminating plants. Other species of interest: *P. fluorescens, P. putida, P. stutzeri, P. mendocina, P. alcaligenes, P. pseudoalcaligenes*.

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- **Very widespread in nature**, a putrefaction agent of animal and plant matter. **It's found in about 15% of healthy people**, in the **gut, armpit and perineum**, preferring moist areas.
- It contaminates **toilets, bathrooms and sinks, especially the drain trap**. **25–40% of hospital sinks studied were contaminated with *P. aeruginosa*.**
- It's been found in **aqueous solutions, including disinfectant solutions and even antibiotic solutions**, because it survives so well in moist environments. **It ranks first among pseudomonads for frequency of isolation, variety of infections, morbidity and mortality.**

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Thin Gram-negative rod**, **1–5 by 0.5–1 µm**, **motile with 1–3 polar flagella** (non-flagellated strains exist), non-sporulating, sometimes with a capsule.
- **Aerobic**, and facultatively anaerobic only through **nitrate respiration**. Not very demanding nutritionally. It **multiplies between 5 and 42 °C, optimally at 30–37 °C**. **Growth at 42 °C separates it from other species** (***P. putida* and *P. fluorescens* grow at 4 °C, never at 42 °C**).
- **Catalase-positive, OXIDASE-POSITIVE, degrades glucose OXIDATIVELY, doesn't ferment lactose.** It produces a **pyocin** (a bacteriocin) that inhibits some strains of the same species.
- **Resistance**: sensitive to heat, phenol and 80° alcohol, but **resistant to quaternary ammonium compounds**. **Hospitals select strains resistant to antiseptics, disinfectants and antibiotics.**

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** The **flagellar H antigen** (protein) and **somatic O antigen** (LPS). Several **serotypes** within the species are described from several O and H factors, useful mainly for epidemiology.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **Pili** mediate attachment to epithelial cells.
- **Proteases** (histotoxic effects), **lipase**, **hemolysins**.
- **Exotoxins, especially exotoxin A.** It's **about 20,000 times more toxic than endotoxin** and *modern layer*: **inhibits protein synthesis by ADP-ribosylating elongation factor 2**.
- **Endotoxin** (the cell-wall LPS).
- **Pigments**: **pyocyanin (blue)** and **pyoverdin** (a fluorescent siderophore).
- **Glycocalyx (alginate).** **Strains from cystic fibrosis patients have a very well-developed glycocalyx**, which **mediates adherence to the respiratory mucosa and prevents opsonization**. In chronic infection in these patients, strains have been shown to **switch from S forms to M (mucoid) forms**, because of mutations identified recently (2008): **mutations in the *mucA* gene, which derepress alginate synthesis**.
- *Modern*: **type III secretion system (ExoU, ExoS, ExoT, ExoY)**; **quorum sensing (las/rhl)**; **biofilm**; pyocyanin as a generator of reactive oxygen species.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- Because of its adaptability, survival and virulence factors, *P. aeruginosa* causes **very varied infections, from superficial skin infections to fulminant septicemia**.
- **In people with normal resistance: localized infections.** These include **folliculitis**, **otitis ("swimmer's ear")** and **eye infections**, after contamination with aqueous solutions or after **swimming in a pool**. **The blue-green color of dressings can signal *P. aeruginosa*.** In the skin: **vesicles that burst and re-form on a necrotic base**. When it progresses deep, you get ***ecthyma gangrenosum***, similar to the lesions in **neutropenic patients**.
- **In hospitalized patients with lowered resistance** who undergo **invasive procedures** (intubation, catheterization), and in **burn patients**: localized infections with **high potential to spread**, leading to **bacteremia, osteomyelitis, meningitis, endocarditis and septicemia**. **80% of *P. aeruginosa* septicemias are fatal.**
- **Chronic lung infection in cystic fibrosis (mucoviscidosis)**, caused by a particular **mucoid** phenotype.

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.**
- **Without ignoring clinical suspicion** (blue or yellow-green fluorescent pus), **the diagnosis is bacteriological**.
- Specimens: **pus, wound or burn secretions, sputum or tracheal aspirate, urine, blood, CSF, ear or eye secretions**. Collect close to onset, before antibiotics, with asepsis.
- **Because carriage is relatively common, you have to separate carriage from real infection.** **Quantitative counts** (urine, sputum) and **isolating the same phage type or serotype** help. **Take special care with cystic fibrosis patients.**

**2. Macro and micro.**
- Macroscopically: the pus looks suggestive, **blue or fluorescent yellow-green**.
- Microscopically: **at least two smears, methylene blue and Gram**, under oil immersion. You'll see skin cells (possibly altered), **inflammatory cells (leukocytes, pus cells)** and **Gram-negative rods of 1–5 by 0.5–1 µm, in short chains, pairs or singly**.

**3. Culture.**
- It grows on ordinary media in **18–24 hours**, at **5–42 °C, optimally 30–37 °C**. **Selective media** (*Pseudomonas* base agar with Cetrimide, Irgasan, C-N or C-F-C) are preferred, and sometimes blood agar.
- **On solid media: S colonies that develop a specific pigment, and the medium becomes pigmented too (blue or fluorescent yellow-green).** Colonies (all S type) can look different from each other, **giving the impression that different bacteria are present**.
- **The culture gives off an aromatic smell, like linden blossom or jasmine.** **On blood agar it's hemolytic.** Many strains are strongly hemolytic, and the medium later turns brown.
- **In broth**: it clouds the medium and forms an **adherent grey pellicle ("membrane") at the surface**. After 18–24 hours the **pigment shows up beneath it**. It often forms a **thick whitish ring of mucoid material stuck to the glass**.
- **On MacConkey or Istrati-Meitert**: small, **lactose-negative** colonies that **stick to the medium**.

**MAYA:** The colony types, Table 32-3.

**ALEX:**
- **Type 1**: large, 2–3 mm, round, with a **domed center, irregular edges and a tendency to spread, the "fried egg" look**. Granular surface, **buttery consistency**, homogeneous suspensions.
- **Type 2**: smaller, convex, like **coliforms**, homogeneous suspensions.
- **Type 3**: convex, **rough**, granular suspensions.
- **Mucoid colonies**: convex at first, then they tend to merge. Some become watery and translucent, others **gummy**. **They come from cystic fibrosis patients and can be confused with *Klebsiella***.
- **Dwarf colonies**: grow after **48–72 hours**, also from cystic fibrosis patients.
- **The culture surface is iridescent, with a metallic sheen and patches of autolysis**, especially when types 1 and 3 predominate. **Types 1–3 often coexist in the same culture.**

**MAYA:** Pigments.

**ALEX:**
- **Pyocyanin (blue)** and **pyoverdin/fluorescein** (yellow-green or yellow-brown, **fluorescent**) give the culture, and gradually the medium, a **bright green** color.
- Less often, other soluble pigments: **pyorubrin (red)** and **pyomelanin (brown, brown-black)**.
- **Some strains produce only pyoverdin.** Both pigments are fluorescent.
- **Mucoid colonies are non-pigmented and made of non-motile bacilli.**
- To stimulate pigment production: **subculture onto King F medium (stimulates fluorescein)** and **King P medium (stimulates pyocyanin)**.
- **Examination under short-wave UV (254 nm)** avoids confusing fluorescent pyoverdin with the **yellow, diffusible but non-fluorescent pigment of *Burkholderia cepacia***.

**4a.** Gram-negative rods, 1–5 by 0.5–1 µm, in short chains, pairs or singly, relatively pleomorphic. **Old cultures may show filamentous or coccoid forms.** Motility is examined on a fresh wet mount. **A single polar flagellum (monotrichous).**

**4b.** As above: colony types 1–3, mucoid, dwarf; pigments; linden or jasmine smell; hemolysis; pellicle in broth; growth at 42 °C.

**4c. Biochemistry.**

**MAYA:** How do you identify a non-pigmented strain?

**ALEX:** The key characters for **achromogenic *P. aeruginosa***:
- motility by **a single polar flagellum**;
- **the characteristic aromatic smell**;
- **growth at 42 °C, but NOT at 4 °C**;
- **oxidation of glucose but never of disaccharides**;
- **nitrate reduction**. It **grows along the stab line in a nitrate agar column**, while ***P. putida* and most *P. fluorescens* don't**;
- it **doesn't tolerate 6.5% NaCl**, while **all *P. stutzeri* and *P. mendocina* strains grow at that concentration**.

- **Oxidase-positive (Annex 2), catalase-positive**, glucose degraded oxidatively. **On TSI both butt and slant stay red.** **An alkaline butt and slant on TSI is a useful clue.** It doesn't ferment lactose. **The Hugh-Leifson glucose oxidation-fermentation test** can be done.

**MAYA:** Species differentiation, Table 32-4.

**ALEX:**
- ***P. aeruginosa***: grows on MacConkey +, **42 °C +, 4 °C –**, **pigment Pc+/Pv+**, NH₃ from arginine +, nitrates reduced +, **gas from nitrates +**, urease d, gelatin d, ONPG –, acid from glucose/fructose/xylose +, mannitol d, sucrose –, lactose –, **1 polar flagellum**.
- ***P. fluorescens***: **42 °C –, 4 °C +**, pigment Pv, **gelatin +**, **more than 1 polar flagellum**.
- ***P. putida***: 42 °C –, 4 °C +, pigment Pv, **gelatin –**, nitrates d.
- ***P. stutzeri***: 42 °C +, **rough, wrinkled, adherent colonies**, a **shiny, non-diffusible brown pigment**, nitrates + with gas, **starch +**.
- ***P. mendocina***: **smooth, flat colonies with a non-diffusible yellow-brown pigment**, hydrolyzes arginine.
- ***P. pseudoalcaligenes***: asaccharolytic, or weakly oxidizes only fructose (19% of strains oxidize glucose, 11% maltose).

**ALEX:** ***P. fluorescens* differs from *P. putida* by growing at 4 °C and hydrolyzing gelatin.** **Old *P. putida* cultures smell of rotten fish.** In the ***P. stutzeri* group**, CDC **Vb-3** organisms are considered a biovar of *P. stutzeri*. **Because rough colonies don't give homogeneous suspensions, commercial galleries are hard to use.**

**MAYA:** The pragmatic rule.

**ALEX:** **For the clinician, where *B. mallei* and *B. pseudomallei* don't circulate, "a pseudomonad other than *P. aeruginosa*" plus the antibiotic susceptibility is enough.** Identifying non-fluorescent pseudomonads and other non-fermenters is **laborious and expensive, and stays the job of reference labs**.
- Commercial systems: e.g. **Rapid NTF**, species ID in 4–48 hours.

**4d. Antigens.** **Serotyping** is an epidemiological marker.
**4e. Pathogenicity.** **It can be studied in some situations: inoculate 4 mice with fixed amounts of bacteria grown on a 2% agar slant and follow their survival for four days.**
**4f. Virulence.** *Modern*: PCR for ***exoU/exoS, lasB, toxA***; assessment of the **mucoid (alginate) phenotype** in cystic fibrosis.
**4g. Phage typing.** Testing **sensitivity to bacteriophages** is a **useful epidemiological marker for *P. aeruginosa***, reserved for reference labs.
**4h. Pyocin typing.** **Pyocin typing**, determining which pyocin type is produced, is a **classic epidemiological marker for *P. aeruginosa***, alongside sero- and phage typing. For *B. cepacia*: sero- and bacteriocin typing.
**4i.** Molecular tests: **nucleotide probes, restriction endonuclease analysis of DNA, PCR amplification of specific chromosomal sequences**, all valuable markers for *P. aeruginosa*. **Ribotyping for *B. cepacia***. *Modern*: MALDI-TOF; **WGS/cgMLST for international high-risk clones (ST235, ST111, ST175)** and for ICU outbreak investigation.

**5. Susceptibility testing.**
- **An antibiogram is mandatory in every *P. aeruginosa* infection**, usually by disk diffusion. **In severe infections, disk diffusion should be supplemented by MIC and MBC.**
- ***P. aeruginosa* is one of the organisms that make choosing etiological treatment hardest**, because it resists so many common antibiotics. Some authors list it as susceptible to mezlocillin, ticarcillin, ticarcillin-clavulanate, piperacillin, piperacillin-tazobactam, imipenem, aztreonam, some aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins (ceftazidime). **But the principle to remember is that the antibiogram remains mandatory.**
- *Modern*, **natural resistance**: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, **cefotaxime/ceftriaxone, ertapenem**, tetracyclines, tigecycline, **trimethoprim, cotrimoxazole**, chloramphenicol, **kanamycin**.
- **Acquired resistance mechanisms**: **chromosomal AmpC hyperproduction**, **loss of the OprD porin (imipenem resistance)**, **efflux pumps (MexAB-OprM)**, **carbapenemases (VIM, IMP, NDM, GES)**, ESBLs. **New options**: ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam, imipenem-relebactam, cefiderocol.

### Serology

**ALEX:** **Not done.** There's a humoral response, with antibodies (opsonins, agglutinins), **but the titers are insignificant and not protective**.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- **Antibiotics, often in combination**, chosen according to the antibiogram.
- **Prevention**:
  - **isolate patients whose natural resistance is severely reduced**;
  - **close and generally disinfect wards with all their contents**, paying **special attention to sinks and bathrooms**, with a disinfectant effective against *P. aeruginosa*;
  - **strict asepsis and antisepsis** in treating wounds and burns, and in every medical or surgical procedure (catheterization, endoscopy);
  - **dry the surfaces**, because moisture helps it multiply.
- **Epidemiology**: colonization is common in hospitalized patients. It spreads by direct or indirect contact (instruments, objects, dressings, medicines). **In skin infections almost anyone can be susceptible. In other situations disease appears only when host resistance drops significantly.**

## PART C — *ACINETOBACTER*

**ALEX:** A note first. **Buiuc chapter 32.2 ("Gram-negative coccobacilli") covers this genus, but that text wasn't in the materials, so the whole section comes from current standard knowledge.** It's all *modern layer*.

### Taxonomy

**ALEX:**
- Family ***Moraxellaceae***. The genus has **over 70 species**.
- Clinically significant: **the *Acinetobacter baumannii-calcoaceticus* complex (Acb)**. It includes ***A. baumannii*** (the most important), *A. pittii* (genomospecies 3), *A. nosocomialis* (genomospecies 13TU) and *A. calcoaceticus* (usually environmental). Others: *A. lwoffii, A. junii, A. haemolyticus, A. ursingii, A. radioresistens*.
- **Old names**: *Herellea vaginicola, Mima polymorpha, Bacterium anitratum, Achromobacter, Moraxella lwoffii*.
- **Phenotypic methods can't reliably separate the Acb complex species**, so labs often report **"*A. baumannii* complex."**

### Habitat

**ALEX:**
- **Ubiquitous**: soil, water, food. Part of the **normal skin flora in 25–40% of healthy people** (moist areas: armpit, groin, between the toes), especially *A. lwoffii* and *A. johnsonii*.
- But ***A. baumannii* is rare on healthy skin** and has an **almost exclusively hospital reservoir**: **dry surfaces, mattresses, ventilation equipment, staff hands**.
- **Exceptional resistance to drying and disinfectants.** **It survives for weeks on inert surfaces**, which explains **long-running ICU outbreaks**.

### General characters

**ALEX:**
- **Short, plump Gram-negative coccobacilli, often in pairs.** **On a smear they can be confused with *Neisseria* or with Gram-positive cocci**, because they **decolorize poorly** in the Gram stain. They're pleomorphic: **rod-shaped in log phase, coccoid in stationary phase**.
- **NON-MOTILE**: they have no flagella. That's where the name comes from: **a-kineto-bacter, "the bacterium that doesn't move."** But they do show **twitching motility** on moist surfaces, via **type IV pili**.
- **Strictly aerobic; non-fermenting.** *A. baumannii* **oxidizes glucose**. Asaccharolytic species such as *A. lwoffii* don't.
- **CATALASE-POSITIVE and, crucially, OXIDASE-NEGATIVE.** **A negative oxidase is the key trait that separates it from *Pseudomonas*** and other oxidase-positive pseudomonads.
- **Don't reduce nitrates (or only weakly); indole-negative**. **They grow on MacConkey** (lactose-negative colonies, sometimes slightly violet). ***A. baumannii* grows at 44 °C**, which separates it from most other species.

### Antigenic structure

**ALEX:** LPS (lipooligosaccharide) with endotoxin activity. **Capsular polysaccharide (K)**, with **over 100 KL types described by sequencing**. Surface antigens were used historically for serotyping, now replaced by molecular typing.

### Virulence factors

**ALEX:**
- **Low intrinsic virulence.** It's an **opportunistic pathogen whose clinical success depends more on antibiotic resistance and environmental persistence than on aggressiveness**.
- **Polysaccharide capsule** (antiphagocytic, complement-resistant). **OmpA protein** (adhesin, causes epithelial cell apoptosis). **Type IV pili.** **Biofilm on catheters and endotracheal tubes.** **Phospholipase D.** **Iron acquisition (acinetobactin).** **Type VI secretion system** (inter-bacterial competition).
- **Resistance to drying and disinfectants** works as an "ecological" virulence factor.

### Clinical significance

**ALEX:**
- **Almost exclusively healthcare-associated infections**, in **ICU patients on mechanical ventilation, with catheters, burns or polytrauma, after broad-spectrum antibiotics**.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is the most common form.** Also **catheter bacteremia**, **wound and burn infections**, **UTIs in catheterized patients**, **post-operative or post-neurosurgical meningitis**, and rarely dialysis peritonitis.
- **War and disaster wound infections**, the **"Iraqibacter."** *A. baumannii* became emblematic of wound infections in wounded soldiers.
- **High attributable mortality**, largely from **delayed appropriate therapy**.
- **Telling colonization from infection is essential.** **Isolation from tracheal aspirate or a wound doesn't automatically mean infection.**

### Direct laboratory diagnosis

**1. Collection.** **Tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage (quantitative)**, blood culture, urine (quantitative), CSF, wound or burn secretions, **catheter tip (Maki technique)**. For outbreak investigations: **environmental swabs** (mattresses, equipment, surfaces) and **patient screening swabs** (rectal, throat, skin, wound).

**2. Micro.** **Plump Gram-negative coccobacilli, often in pairs, that decolorize incompletely** and **can be wrongly reported as Gram-positive diplococci or as *Neisseria***. Leukocytes confirm the quality of a respiratory specimen.

**3. Culture.**
- **Grows easily on blood agar, chocolate agar and MacConkey** (unlike many other demanding non-fermenters), aerobically, at 35–37 °C, in 24 hours.
- **Colonies are smooth, opaque, non-pigmented, 1–2 mm, slightly domed and "creamy."** **On MacConkey they're lactose-negative but often pale violet** (some strains oxidize lactose). ***A. haemolyticus* and *A. junii* are hemolytic** on blood agar. **The *A. baumannii* complex is NON-hemolytic.**
- **Selective screening media**: **Leeds Acinetobacter Medium (LAM)**, **CHROMagar Acinetobacter** (± supplements for MDR or carbapenem-resistant strains), and **MacConkey with imipenem**.
- **Incubation at 44 °C favors the *A. baumannii* complex.**

**4a.** Pleomorphic Gram-negative coccobacilli in pairs, **non-motile**, non-sporulating, capsulated.
**4b.** Smooth, non-pigmented, non-hemolytic (Acb) colonies, lactose-negative on MacConkey; growth at 44 °C.

**4c. Biochemistry.**
- **The preliminary identification key**: a **Gram-negative rod or coccobacillus that's non-fermenting, OXIDASE-NEGATIVE, catalase-positive, non-motile and grows on MacConkey**. That combination separates *Acinetobacter* from ***Pseudomonas*** (oxidase +, motile) and from ***Stenotrophomonas*** (oxidase –, but **motile**, **lysine decarboxylase +, DNase +**).
- **Additional tests**: nitrates –, indole –, urease – (Acb), growth at 37, 41 and 44 °C, **glucose oxidation (+ in *A. baumannii*, – in *A. lwoffii*)**, hemolysis, citrate use.
- **Biochemical galleries and automated systems reliably identify only the "*A. baumannii* complex," not individual species.**

**4d.** Serotyping (O/K antigens) is historical, replaced by molecular methods.
**4e.** Not done.
**4f.** PCR for ***ompA, csuE, bap***; biofilm assessment.
**4g.** Phage typing is historical, abandoned in favor of molecular typing.
**4h.** **Molecular typing**: **PFGE** (classic), **MLST (Oxford and Pasteur schemes)** with the **international clones IC1, IC2 (the most widespread, ST2 Pasteur) and IC3**, **rep-PCR (DiversiLab)**, and **capsular KL/OCL typing**.
**4i.**
- **MALDI-TOF is the routine method.** It reliably identifies the genus and the Acb complex, and **with dedicated databases it can separate *A. baumannii* from *A. pittii*/*A. nosocomialis***.
- **Sequencing of *bla*OXA-51-like**, the **intrinsic marker of *A. baumannii*** (naturally present only in this species). **PCR for *bla*OXA-51 is the classic molecular test for confirming the species.**
- ***rpoB* sequencing, ARDRA, WGS/cgMLST** for exact species identification and outbreak investigation.
- Rapid molecular and immunochromatographic tests for **carbapenemases (OXA-23, OXA-24/40, OXA-58, NDM)**.

**5. Susceptibility testing.**
- Disk diffusion per EUCAST (its own *Acinetobacter* table). **Broth microdilution MIC is mandatory for colistin**. **Gradient tests and disk diffusion aren't valid for polymyxins.**
- **Natural resistance**: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, **aztreonam, ertapenem, trimethoprim, chloramphenicol, fosfomycin**.
- **Acquired resistance is the central problem**:
  - **class D carbapenemases (OXA-23, OXA-24/40, OXA-58, OXA-143)**;
  - **metallo-β-lactamases (NDM, VIM, IMP)**;
  - **hyperproduction of the intrinsic ADC cephalosporinase**;
  - porin loss;
  - **efflux pumps (AdeABC)**;
  - aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes and **16S rRNA methylases (armA)**.
  - **XDR/PDR strains are common in Romanian ICUs.**
- **Treatment options**: **sulbactam** (intrinsic activity against *Acinetobacter*) and **sulbactam-durlobactam**, **colistin, cefiderocol, minocycline/tigecycline**, often in combination. **Carbapenems only if the strain is susceptible.**

### Serology

**ALEX:** **Not done.** Diagnosis is **exclusively bacteriological (direct)**. There are no clinically useful serological tests.

### Treatment and prevention

**ALEX:**
- According to the antibiogram, **often combination therapy** for carbapenem-resistant strains.
- **Prevention and control**: **hand hygiene**, **contact precautions and cohorting of colonized patients**, **thorough cleaning and disinfection of surfaces and equipment**. Because it resists drying, **the environment is the main reservoir**. Also **carriage screening in outbreaks** and **limiting carbapenems through antibiotic stewardship programs**.

### Annex 3 — Orientation key: fermenters vs non-fermenters

**MAYA:** This is the big summary grid. It covers Enterobacterales, *V. cholerae*, *Aeromonas*, *Plesiomonas*, *P. aeruginosa*, *A. baumannii*, *S. maltophilia* and *C. jejuni*.

**ALEX:** Row by row:

- **Glucose metabolism**: fermentative for Enterobacterales, *Vibrio*, *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas*. **Oxidative** for *P. aeruginosa*, *A. baumannii* (asaccharolytic in other species) and *S. maltophilia*. ***Campylobacter* doesn't attack carbohydrates at all.**
- **Oxidase**: **negative** for Enterobacterales, ***A. baumannii*** and ***S. maltophilia***. **Positive** for *Vibrio, Aeromonas, Plesiomonas, P. aeruginosa, C. jejuni*.
- **Catalase**: positive for all.
- **Motility and flagella**: Enterobacterales are peritrichous or non-motile. *Vibrio, Aeromonas, Plesiomonas* are polar monotrichous. *P. aeruginosa* has 1–3 polar flagella. ***A. baumannii* is non-motile and non-flagellated.** ***S. maltophilia* is polar lophotrichous.** *C. jejuni* has a single polar flagellum, with darting motility.
- **Growth on MacConkey**: yes for Enterobacterales, *Aeromonas*, *Plesiomonas*, *P. aeruginosa* (lactose –), *A. baumannii* (lactose –) and *S. maltophilia*. Only some *Vibrio* species. ***C. jejuni* doesn't.**
- **Atmosphere**: Enterobacterales, *Vibrio*, *Aeromonas* and *Plesiomonas* are aerobic and facultatively anaerobic. *P. aeruginosa* is strictly aerobic (except nitrate respiration). *A. baumannii* and *S. maltophilia* are strictly aerobic. ***C. jejuni* is microaerophilic, 5% O₂.**
- **Diagnostically useful temperature**: 35–37 °C for Enterobacterales. 35–37 °C and pH 8.6–9.5 for *Vibrio*. 35–37 °C for *Aeromonas*. 8–45 °C for *Plesiomonas*. ***P. aeruginosa* grows at 42 °C but not 4 °C.** ***A. baumannii* grows at 44 °C.** 35 °C for *S. maltophilia*. ***C. jejuni* grows at 42 °C but not at 25 °C.**
- **Selective medium of choice**: MacConkey, ADCL, XLD, SS for Enterobacterales. TCBS, BSA and APW for *Vibrio*. CIN blood agar or ampicillin for *Aeromonas*. MacConkey for *Plesiomonas*. *Pseudomonas* base agar + cetrimide for *P. aeruginosa*. LAM or CHROMagar *Acinetobacter* for *A. baumannii*. **VIA or imipenem agar for *S. maltophilia*.** Skirrow, CCDA or Preston under microaerophilia for *C. jejuni*.
- **Pigment**: rare in Enterobacterales (*Serratia* red, *Enterobacter* yellow). None for *Vibrio*. Brown for *A. salmonicida*. None for *Plesiomonas*. Pyocyanin plus pyoverdin for *P. aeruginosa*. None for *A. baumannii*. **Yellow-greenish for *S. maltophilia*.** None for *C. jejuni*.

### Annex 6 — Natural resistances (EUCAST expected phenotypes)

**MAYA:** The whole table is an addition beyond the course materials, right?

**ALEX:** Yes, the whole table. **Its purpose is quality control: a strain reported "susceptible" to an antibiotic its species is naturally resistant to signals an identification or testing error.**

- ***E. coli***: practically none. **Any resistance is acquired**: ESBL CTX-M-15 (ST131), plasmid AmpC, carbapenemases, *mcr-1*.
- ***Klebsiella pneumoniae/oxytoca***: ampicillin, amoxicillin, ticarcillin (SHV-1/OXY). **Never report "ampicillin susceptible."**
- ***Enterobacter, C. freundii, Serratia, Morganella, Providencia, Hafnia***: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first-generation cephalosporins, cefoxitin (**inducible AmpC**). There's a **derepression risk with third-generation cephalosporins, so prefer cefepime or carbapenems** in severe infections.
- ***C. koseri***: aminopenicillins, ticarcillin (penicillinase). It **stays susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanate and first-generation cephalosporins**.
- ***Proteus* (all species)**: tetracyclines, tigecycline, colistin, nitrofurantoin. **No nitrofurantoin for *Proteus* UTIs.**
- ***P. vulgaris, P. penneri***: also aminopenicillins and cefuroxime (class A "K1"). ***P. mirabilis* is ampicillin-susceptible.**
- ***Morganella, Providencia***: colistin, tetracyclines, tigecycline, nitrofurantoin. ***P. stuartii/P. rettgeri* are also resistant to gentamicin and tobramycin** (AAC(2′)-Ia). **Amikacin usually stays active.**
- ***Serratia marcescens***: also colistin. **Don't use colistin.**
- ***Salmonella, Shigella***: aminoglycosides, first- and second-generation cephalosporins and cephamycins look active *in vitro* but are **clinically ineffective**, because of intracellular survival. **Don't report them as susceptible.** Test ciprofloxacin (pefloxacin screen), ceftriaxone, azithromycin, cotrimoxazole.
- ***Y. enterocolitica***: aminopenicillins, ticarcillin, first-generation cephalosporins (β-lactamases A and B). Active: cotrimoxazole, FQs, third-generation cephalosporins, aminoglycosides.
- ***Campylobacter jejuni/coli***: trimethoprim, sulfonamides, vancomycin, rifampicin, aztreonam. **Drug of choice: azithromycin.** FQ resistance from *gyrA* is over 50% in some countries.
- ***Aeromonas***: ampicillin (except *A. trota*), amoxicillin-clavulanate. Multiple inducible β-lactamases including **CphA carbapenemase**. **Avoid β-lactam monotherapy.**
- ***P. aeruginosa***: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, **cefotaxime/ceftriaxone, ertapenem**, tetracyclines, tigecycline, trimethoprim, cotrimoxazole, chloramphenicol. **Active**: ceftazidime, cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, imipenem/meropenem, aztreonam, aminoglycosides, ciprofloxacin, colistin.
- ***A. baumannii***: aminopenicillins, amoxicillin-clavulanate, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, **aztreonam, ertapenem**, trimethoprim, chloramphenicol, fosfomycin (ADC + OXA-51-like + impermeability). Options: **sulbactam (± durlobactam), colistin, cefiderocol, minocycline**.
- ***S. maltophilia***: **all carbapenems**, most β-lactams, aminoglycosides, aztreonam (**L1 MBL + L2 cephalosporinase**). **Drug of choice: cotrimoxazole**, with minocycline and levofloxacin as alternatives.
- ***B. cepacia* complex**: aminoglycosides, colistin, aminopenicillins, first- and second-generation cephalosporins. Active: cotrimoxazole, meropenem, ceftazidime, minocycline.

## THE RAPID-FIRE QUIZ

**ALEX:** Okay, Maya. Thirty questions, one-word or one-line answers. Go.

**ALEX:** 1. Four defining biochemical traits of Enterobacterales?
**MAYA:** Ferment glucose, oxidase-negative, catalase-positive, reduce nitrates to nitrites.

**ALEX:** 2. More than what percentage of normal gut flora is anaerobic?
**MAYA:** Over 90%.

**ALEX:** 3. Which antigen is heat-stable and agglutinates slowly and granularly?
**MAYA:** O. H is heat-labile and agglutinates fast and fluffy.

**ALEX:** 4. How are H suspensions preserved?
**MAYA:** 5‰ formalin. Except *Serratia*, where H antigens are prepared with 6‰ phenol.

**ALEX:** 5. Name the six diarrheagenic *E. coli* pathotypes.
**MAYA:** ETEC, EHEC/STEC, EIEC, EAggEC, EPEC, DAEC.

**ALEX:** 6. Which medium for O157, and what do its colonies look like?
**MAYA:** Sorbitol MacConkey. Colorless, sorbitol-negative colonies.

**ALEX:** 7. Why no antibiotics in EHEC?
**MAYA:** They induce the lambdoid phage, cause massive toxin release and raise the risk of HUS.

**ALEX:** 8. Urine transport rule?
**MAYA:** 30 minutes, otherwise +4 °C.

**ALEX:** 9. The UTI threshold?
**MAYA:** ≥10⁵ CFU/ml. Or 10⁴–10⁵ with leukocyturia and symptoms. Or any count from a suprapubic puncture.

**ALEX:** 10. The only *Klebsiella* that's indole-positive?
**MAYA:** *K. oxytoca*.

**ALEX:** 11. The only malonate-negative *Klebsiella*?
**MAYA:** *K. ozaenae*.

**ALEX:** 12. What does a string test over 5 mm mean?
**MAYA:** Hypermucoviscous, hypervirulent *K. pneumoniae*.

**ALEX:** 13. *Cronobacter sakazakii*: source and mortality?
**MAYA:** Powdered infant formula. 40–80% mortality from neonatal meningitis or necrotizing enterocolitis.

**ALEX:** 14. Which *Serratia* trait trap mimics *S.* Typhi?
**MAYA:** On TSI/MILF: no or little gas, no H₂S, lactose –, motile, indole –, LDC +, PAD –. You separate them with ODC, DNase, gelatinase, VP, adonitol, sorbitol and arabinose.

**ALEX:** 15. Three *Proteus* culture phenomena?
**MAYA:** Swarming in concentric waves, the Dienes demarcation line, and the climbing phenomenon in condensation water.

**ALEX:** 16. *P. mirabilis* versus *P. vulgaris* on indole, ODC and maltose?
**MAYA:** *Mirabilis*: indole –, ODC +, maltose –. *Vulgaris*: the opposite. And the scanned table in the book has those rows swapped.

**ALEX:** 17. *Morganella* versus *Providencia*?
**MAYA:** *Morganella* is ODC + and citrate –. *Providencia* is ODC –, citrate + and malonate +. Neither swarms.

**ALEX:** 18. Specimen chronology in typhoid?
**MAYA:** Blood in week one. Then stool, urine, bile and bone marrow.

**ALEX:** 19. The *S.* Typhi antigenic formula?
**MAYA:** O 9,12,Vi; H d; no phase 2. Group D1.

**ALEX:** 20. Widal-type tube series and suggestive titers?
**MAYA:** TO, AO, BO and d, a, b. 52 °C, 18–20 h for O and 2 h for H. 1/250 for O, 1/2,500 for H, a fourfold rise 7–10 days apart. Anti-Vi over 1/40 in carriers. Useless in vaccinated people.

**ALEX:** 21. Three typhoid vaccines?
**MAYA:** Vi polysaccharide i.m., oral live Ty21a, and the inactivated whole-cell vaccine, which is the most reactogenic. 50–80% protection.

**ALEX:** 22. *Shigella* infective dose, and do we enrich?
**MAYA:** About 100 bacteria. No enrichment, we plate directly.

**ALEX:** 23. Which *Shigella* subgroup is mannitol-negative?
**MAYA:** Subgroup A, *S. dysenteriae*.

**ALEX:** 24. *Yersinia* motility rule?
**MAYA:** Motile at 22–30 °C, non-motile at 37 °C. *Y. pestis* is always non-motile.

**ALEX:** 25. The *Y. pestis* colony, and the *Y. enterocolitica* colony on CIN?
**MAYA:** *Y. pestis* makes "Chinese hat" colonies. On CIN you get a bull's eye with a red center.

**ALEX:** 26. Cholera stool microscopy: what do you NOT see?
**MAYA:** Leukocytes. Cholera isn't invasive.

**ALEX:** 27. *V. cholerae* on TCBS, and why you don't do oxidase straight from TCBS?
**MAYA:** Yellow colonies. Sucrose on TCBS can give a false-positive oxidase.

**ALEX:** 28. *Aeromonas* versus *Vibrio*, in three tests?
**MAYA:** *Aeromonas* is O/129-resistant, doesn't grow at 6% NaCl, and is string-test negative.

**ALEX:** 29. *C. jejuni* versus *C. coli*?
**MAYA:** Hippurate hydrolysis is positive in *jejuni*.

**ALEX:** 30. Why can't serology confirm *H. pylori* eradication, and what do you use instead?
**MAYA:** Antibodies persist for months or years. Use the urea breath test or stool antigen at least 4 weeks after treatment, and 2 weeks off PPIs.

**ALEX:** Bonus round. What's the single test that separates *Acinetobacter* from *Pseudomonas*?
**MAYA:** Oxidase. *Acinetobacter* is negative, *Pseudomonas* is positive. And *Acinetobacter* is non-motile.

**ALEX:** And the drug of choice for *Stenotrophomonas*?
**MAYA:** Cotrimoxazole, because the L1 metallo-β-lactamase kills every carbapenem.

**ALEX:** And what does Weil-Felix diagnose?
**MAYA:** Rickettsial diseases, not *Proteus*.

**ALEX:** Thirty out of thirty, plus the bonus. You're ready.

**ALEX:** Don't plate *Shigella* on Wilson-Blair, and don't give nitrofurantoin for *Proteus*. Goodbye!
